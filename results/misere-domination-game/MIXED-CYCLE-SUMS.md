# Misère Domination Game

**All even cycles together with cycles of orders 3, 5, 7 and 9.** In any sum consisting of these untouched cycles and ordinary bordered runs, replace C(3) and C(7) by one heap of size one. Replace C(5) and C(9) by two heaps of size two. Drop C(4) and C(8). The remaining even cycles act exactly as in the [even-cycle theorem](EVEN-CYCLE-SUMS.md), with the parity test applied after those replacements.

In particular, for unions of these cycles without initial runs, let a count the C(3) and C(7) components. If there is a C(5), C(9), or even cycle of order other than four and eight, the second player wins iff a is even. Otherwise the second player wins iff a is odd. Arbitrary untouched odd cycles are not covered; the last section gives a precise obstruction.

## The small odd cycle replacements

The sole option of C(3) is the empty heap game. The sole option of C(7) is H(4), which is universally neutral by the [reply proof](EVEN-CYCLE-SUMS.md). Accordingly both are equivalent to H(1) in any sum. To justify the option replacement: if two games' options match up to equivalence under all contexts, induction over any added game's remaining options shows their sums have the same outcomes.

The single option of C(5) is H(2); that of C(9) is H(6). Both children have even actual length and one type-E virtual pile of weight one. Treat each such unopened cycle as two temporary weight-one even heaps, meaning two copies of H(2) in the formula. These temporary heaps are not real individual moves. A genuine move in the cycle replaces both with a single even weight-one heap, a transformation equivalent in the formula to deleting one temporary pile. All other moves are ordinary heap moves (or the other cycles already classified).

To prove the replacement without any active even cycles, induct on the total remaining vertices of the original position. If the pure-heap formula after replacement says P, the actual total of the pure proxy position is even while any temporary pair is present. Playing a cycle is equivalent to deleting one entire H(2), a legal move of the pure game, and hence gives N. Every real heap move is also a legal move of the proxy game. For any proxy N of even total, a winning move of the pure game cannot be trimming a temporary H(2) down to H(1): it leaves odd actual total and another bad heap, hence N. Thus a genuine move to P exists.

It remains to handle odd total when a temporary pair is present. The proxy position is N by the pure-heap formula. Let X be the true heaps (including the size-one equivalents already handled). There are K temporary unit piles with K positive and even. We show that a real heap of X has an odd-token move to an even P-position, so no forbidden temporary trim is needed.

Apply the [odd-position proof](PROOF.md#proof-the-odd-position-rule) with the K temporary units included only in the sums of virtual weights. Whenever the even reference formula for the current virtual configuration says P, the moves given there start at an odd heap, necessarily a real one in X. Whenever the reference says N and W is odd, there is a real virtual pile since K is even. If a pile is at the positive borderline D=t−2 (or is the type-O special D=3), it must be a real one; choose that move. Otherwise choose any real virtual pile for the decrement. As before, if W=3 and weights are 2+1 one should choose the heavier pile; here K≥2 prevents this special two-pile case. The resulting state in each case is even and P under the proxy formula.

If the reference says N and W is even, there is a dominant pile, necessarily real. If another real virtual pile exists, use it for the odd-token decrement of a nondominant pile. Otherwise the dominant pile of weight c is the only real virtual pile. Its dominance is D=c−K and c is even. For type O, D≥4, so trimming three changes its type to E and gives odd virtual total with new dominance D−1≥3, a P state. For type E and D≥6, trim one to change it to type O, obtaining odd virtual total with dominance at least five, likewise P. The only remaining cases are type E with D=2 or 4. The actual heap has length h=2c+4 and c≥K+2≥4. Remove three and split into 3 and h−6. The resulting virtual weights are one and c−3; the total virtual weight decreases by two, and the remaining largest pile has dominance at most D−4≤0. The unit piles also meet the even central inequalities. Hence this even child is P. The induction establishes the two-heap replacement for arbitrary numbers of both C(5) and C(9).

If an active even cycle remains, use the even-even test of the [even-cycle theorem](EVEN-CYCLE-SUMS.md) on the ordinary heaps plus K temporary units. A move in C(5) or C(9) flips virtual parity but not actual parity; a move in an active even cycle creates an odd bad heap, so from P it reaches N even if it removes the last active cycle. For a position failing the even-even test, the parity-fixing moves in the even-cycle proof can all be played on real heaps: odd actual parity guarantees a real odd heap, and whenever virtual parity is odd a real bad pile exists because K is even. This proves the combined claim.

## Why the same compression does not just cover longer odd cycles

In the pure heap universe, (19) and (3,16) have equal [exact quotient triples](EXACT-QUOTIENT.md): odd actual total, virtual total seven, and peak two. Yet in the larger graph-game universe, adding one untouched C(11) distinguishes them. This follows by the defining minimax recurrence, which the independent checker verifies. Thus the pure quotient **is not** a congruence for all unstarted cycles. Any solution that substitutes the three-coordinate pure profile while an arbitrary cycle remains needs an additional invariant.

## Every longer odd cycle requires a genuinely new quotient class

Let J(c) be C(2c+7) for c≥2. Its sole option is a pure even heap of virtual weight c, namely H(2c+4). Put k copies of H(2) in the context and allow zero or one H(1). Write f(k),g(k) for P-indicators without and with H(1), respectively.

Two unit heaps together are neutral by the reply lemma. A move on a length-two context heap therefore reaches either of the two star parities at k−1, no matter which parity was present. If either outcome at k−1 is P, both outcomes at k are N. Otherwise, at k, the position without a star is P exactly when the ordinary heap outcome after opening the cycle is N. For the position with a star, that same cycle move produces an odd actual total with a bad heap, hence N. Its additional star move goes to the no-star position at k. Thus exactly one of the two outcome indicators is P in this case. At k=0 the two indicators are also opposite by the same direct argument. Therefore both positions are N at odd k, while at even k their outcomes are opposite.

For even k the pure-heap formula after opening the cycle without a star gives P exactly when k≥c if c is even, or k≤c−3 if c is odd; the stated tests also apply at k=0 by the solo rule. In particular:
- if c is even, f(k) is P at even k<c and g(k) is P at even k≥c;
- if c is odd, g(k) is P at even k<c−1 and f(k) is P at even k≥c−1.

These threshold profiles distinguish every pair of odd cycles of order at least eleven. They also show none is equivalent to any pure heap position. If c≥4 is even, the very large even-k tests force an ordinary representative to have odd actual parity (since its sum with a star and many bad heaps is P). But the no-star test at k=2<c is P while an ordinary odd position containing the two bad context heaps must be N. If c≥5 is odd, the very large even-k tests instead force even actual parity. Now the star test at k=2<c−1 produces the opposite contradiction.

The remaining two values c=2 and c=3 are ruled out by loss of pure-heap equivalence. The C(11) distinction between (19) and (3,16) was given above. C(13) distinguishes the same pair of positions with a unit heap adjoined, namely (1,19) and (1,3,16). Since pure equivalence is preserved against every pure context, these distinctions preclude any pure replacement. Thus every unstarted odd cycle beyond length nine adds its own class even when tested only against pure heaps.
