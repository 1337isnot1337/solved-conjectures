# Reconstruction after independent subdivision at a branch vertex

Let a finite tree have a vertex of degree $`r\ge3`$. Delete the vertex and root every resulting arm at its former neighbor. Strip successive unary roots from each arm until the root has zero or at least two children. Call the remaining rooted tree its cap. Write $`L`$ for the largest cap order minus one, $`a_i`$ for the arm orders and $`a`$ for their minimum.

**Theorem.** If $`a\ge\max(3,L+1,2rL)`$, the ordinary chromatic symmetric function distinguishes this tree among all finite trees. Neither the vertex, its degree, the arm orders, the cap shapes nor their assignments are promised for competitors. Consequently, for any fixed tree and branch vertex, subdividing every incident edge independently a sufficiently large number of times produces a tree characterized by its ordinary chromatic symmetric function. No relation between those numbers is required. The general conjecture remains open.

Codex obtained the proof in the WadeDavis research session. It uses Stanley’s spanning-forest expansion and the Martin–Morin–Wagner degree-sequence theorem. [Independent review and source comparison](HUB-SUBDIVISIONS-REVIEW.md) · [Stanley’s original question](https://math.mit.edu/~rstan/pubs/pubfiles/100.pdf)

## Rooted algebra

By Stanley's power-sum expansion the ordinary invariant gives the forest polynomial $`U_T`$, counting each edge-subset forest by the monomial of component orders. The sign in the power sum is fixed by the number of components. Let $`P_C`$ count the same forests in a rooted tree with its root component omitted and $`Q_C=P_C+U_C`$. Use weight $`i`$ on $`x_i`$, truncation $`\tau_k`$ to weight $`k`$, and $`X=\sum x_i`$, $`H=(1-X)^{-1}`$. Then $`P_C=\prod Q_E`$ over the branches and $`U_C=[XP_C]_{|C|}`$ by restoring the omitted component. For a rooted tree of size $`t`$ put $`G_C=\tau_t((1-X)Q_C)`$. Hence $`Q_C=\tau_t(HG_C)`$ and the weight-$`t`$ term of $`G_C`$ vanishes. A new unary root fixes $`G_C`$ by the recurrence. A nonunary cap of order $`d+1\ge3`$ has no proper fringe of order $`d`$, giving $`G_C=-x_d+`$ terms involving only smaller-index variables, with total weighted degree $`d`$. This follows since $`[x_d]P_C=0`$ and $`P_C`$ has constant 1. Such a factor is irreducible, normalized by constant 1. The one-vertex cap gives $`G_C=1`$. The homogeneous generators $`y_i=[H]_i`$ satisfy $`y_i=x_i+`$ smaller indices, so provide an invertible triangular change of variables, with $`H=1+\sum y_i`$. To recover the cap from its normalized factor, determine its order $`d+1`$ from degree (or one from the unit), restore $`Q_C=\tau_{d+1}(HG_C)`$, delete the top weight to get $`P_C`$, and recursively factor $`P_C`$ into its branch $`Q_E`$. Each $`Q_E`$ is monic linear in the largest variable $`x_{|E|}`$ and otherwise uses smaller indices; unique factorization and its constant 1 give the rooted branches. These are the rooted identities used in the earlier [balanced-arm reconstruction](BALANCED-ARMS.md). [Rooted-polynomial source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08186)

## Detecting the caps and recognizing the hub

Put $`m=rL`$, $`M=L+1`$, and $`K=\max(3,M,m)`$. Then $`K\le a`$ and $`2K\lt n=1+\sum a_i`$. Root each tree provisionally at any centroid. Every nonroot descendant fringe is contained in a centroid branch of order at most $`n/2`$. For $`1\le i\le K`$, the coefficient of $`x_i x_{n-i}`$ in $`U_T`$ therefore counts exactly the vertices with fringe order $`i`$, denoted $`f_i`$, independently of the centroid choice. In the target, the original hub may differ from this centroid. For these small orders the counts still agree with rooting at the original hub, because the component containing that hub after a single edge cut has size at least $`n-b=1+\sum_{j\ne\mathrm{max}}a_j>K`$, where $`b=\max a_i`$. Each arm has a unary stem down to its cap of size at most $`M`$, and has size at least $`K`$. Thus for $`B=M,K`$,
```math
f_B=r,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{B}f_i=rB .
```
The same equalities hold in any competitor with the same invariant. Its $`r`$ descendant fringes of order $`B`$ are disjoint, since descendant sets are laminar and a strict descendant has smaller order. All $`rB`$ vertices in their union have fringe order at most $`B`$. They exhaust such vertices, so in particular contain every leaf (a centroid has degree at least two here). For $`M\lt K`$ each of the $`r`$ disjoint $`K`$-blocks contains an $`M`$-block, by tracing any leaf in the former and using laminarity. There is exactly one in each by counting. Every vertex of that $`K`$-block outside its unique $`M`$-block lies on the connecting unary stem, as any offshoot has an uncovered leaf. For $`K=M`$ the two block families coincide. Each $`K`$-block consequently has a cap of order at most $`M`$.

From $`U_T`$ recover $`\tau_K P`$ at any centroid by erasing the unique component larger than $`n/2`$ in each forest with off-root weight at most $`K`$. For a descendant subtree outside the $`K`$-blocks the full order is greater than $`K`$. Its top-weight term $`U_C`$ vanishes through this cutoff, so in computing $`\tau_K P`$ every such $`Q_C`$ can be replaced by the product of its children's factors. Iteration leaves exactly the product of the $`r`$ block factors $`Q_B`$. Multiplying by $`(1-X)^r`$ recovers $`\tau_K\prod G_B=\prod G_B`$, since each factor has degree at most $`L`$ and $`K\ge rL`$. Unique factorization therefore gives the exact multiset of caps, including unit factors by subtraction from $`r`$. This argument also applies to the target, where the $`K`$-blocks are exactly its known distal arm segments, since they are the unique small sides of the corresponding cut edges.

The ordinary chromatic symmetric function determines the full degree sequence of a tree. [Martin, Morin and Wagner, Corollary 5](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0609339) Each reconstructed cap determines its vertices' degrees in the ambient tree because every vertex has degree one plus its number of children, including its root because it has one edge to the exterior. The other vertices of its $`K`$-block have degree two. Subtract these histograms from the common histogram. The target's remaining vertices have exactly one vertex of degree $`r`$ and all others degree two. The same holds for the competitor. The remaining tree is connected, as deleting an entire pendant fringe preserves connectivity. The $`r`$ disjoint blocks attach by one edge each. On contracting them to leaves the resulting tree therefore has just one branch vertex and all other internal vertices have degree two. This forces one block per arm of that branch vertex. Thus every competitor has the common hub structure with these same cap types, and each arm has order at least $`K`$. This conclusion did not require the original hub to be a centroid.

## Recovering arm orders without a marked vertex

Write $`b`$ and $`c`$ for the largest and second largest arm orders, with multiplicity; let $`D=n-1-b`$, the sum of all orders except one largest. For $`K\le i\lt n/2`$ let $`h_i`$ be the coefficient of $`x_i x_{n-i}`$ in the ordinary polynomial. For $`F(i)=\#\{j:a_j\ge i\}`$ the bare arm stems give
```math
h_i=F(i)+F(n-i).
```
Indeed, off-hub fringe subtrees of either indicated order are in bijection with arms at least that long once the indicated order is at least $`K`$. Thus when $`i+1\lt n/2`$, the difference $`h_i-h_{i+1}`$ is the multiplicity of arm order $`i`$ minus that of $`n-1-i`$. The second term vanishes for $`i\lt D`$. In particular it vanishes through $`i=c`$, because $`D-c`$ is the sum of the other $`r-2`$ arms and is positive. Also $`2c+2\lt n`$ in the target and every competitor because $`n\ge1+2c+(r-2)K`$ and $`K\ge3`$. Therefore, scanning these differences upwards starting at $`K`$ gives the first $`r-1`$ arm orders, truncating the final multiplicity if the maximum is tied. Subtracting their sum from $`n-1`$ gives the last order. The procedure works identically on every competitor.

## Matching each cap to its length

Now root at the intrinsically recognized hub. Every component after any cuts that avoids this vertex lies in one arm, hence has at most $`b`$ vertices. Erasing a unique component larger than $`b`$ therefore recovers $`\tau_D P`$ at the actual hub, even if the hub is far from a centroid. If off-root weight is at most $`D`$, the root component has order at least $`b+1`$. If there are no nonconstant caps the arm orders already determine the tree.

Use $`H=1+\sum y_i`$. For any current largest arm order $`j`$, a size-$`j`$ factor $`Q=\tau_j(HG)`$ involves $`y_j`$ only in the monic summand $`y_j`$, because $`j>L`$; smaller factors are independent of $`y_j`$. Set $`H'=H|_{y_j=0}`$. From a product of such factors, by logarithmic differentiation and truncation, we obtain
```math
\tau_m\left(H' \left.\frac{\partial \log P}{\partial y_j}\right|_{y_j=0}\right)
=\tau_m\sum_{a_i=j}G_i^{-1}
```
whenever $`j+m\le D`$ and $`m\le j`$. For each contributing $`Q`$, the derivative is 1 and $`Q|_{y_j=0}`$ equals $`H'G`$ through weight $`m`$. Inversion of series with constant 1 and removal of known factors preserves each available cutoff. The polynomial $`W`$ that is the product of the distinct nonunit cap factors has degree at most $`m`$. Multiplying this truncated identity by $`W`$ recovers the complete polynomial $`uW+\sum_G u_G W/G`$, where $`u,u_G`$ are the counts of the unit and the indicated nonunit caps at this order. These polynomials are linearly independent. Reduction modulo each irreducible $`G`$ forces its coefficient to vanish in any relation, then so does the coefficient of $`W`$. This identifies the cap counts at size $`j`$.

Since $`D-c\ge(r-2)a\ge2m`$, two cases suffice. If $`b-c\lt m`$, then $`D-b\ge m`$ and we may proceed downwards from the largest size, removing each recovered full group of $`Q`$'s. If $`b-c\ge m`$ and $`m>0`$, there is one strictly largest arm. Keep that still unknown factor throughout and proceed downwards through all other sizes $`j\le c`$. We have $`j+m\le\min(D,b)`$. Through weight $`j+m`$ its factor is $`HG_{\rm big}`$. This $`G_{\rm big}`$ is independent of $`y_j`$, so its contribution to the displayed left side is precisely 1 through weight $`m`$. Subtract that known contribution, identify and remove the caps at $`j`$, and continue. The single leftover cap belongs to the largest arm by elimination. Reconstruct each rooted arm by extending its cap with unary roots to the assigned total order. Joining them at the hub proves the theorem.

The hypotheses concern only the minimum arm order and fixed cap sizes. Subdivision of edges incident at the chosen vertex adds unary roots and changes no cap. Thus a single finite lower bound on the added lengths works for all independent choices above it, including the case where one arm is longer than all the others together.
