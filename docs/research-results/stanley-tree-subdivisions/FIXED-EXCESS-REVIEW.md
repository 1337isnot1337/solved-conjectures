The reduction and its finite bound pass. It is an effective reduction for positive excess. No explicit bound on practical run time or affirmative decision for any particular positive excess follows.

1. A retained full-rank cell contains a point with rationally independent target lengths in its projection. Distinct nonempty paths of a tree have different edge sets; hence all reduced target pair lengths are visible there. The competitor's pair count forces its vertex count to be at least that of the target. Its upper bound follows also over the reals by evaluating the defect-weighted exponential sum at zero: twice its value is four minus the sum of squared defects.
2. The entire matching subspace retains the weighted identity. The first row type keeps every old class tied (including invisible classes) and the second, inductively by number of path edges, identifies each representative with the prescribed sum of edge representatives. An order crossing can merge already balanced weights but cannot destroy the equality. The rank bound follows by surjectivity of the edge-representative map onto the target length space, shown by the original full-dimensional cell. A basis of at most delta rows meets at most six pendant edges per row. The high-degree bound uses the *competitor's* excess; the two exceptional sets of leaves need not be disjoint.
3. Vary a remaining trivalent pendant edge on the full subspace. Differentiating the exponential identity in its *exponential parameter* at zero yields equality of total edge lengths, since each single-edge cut divides the total signed defect into two sums of minus one. Each target edge, being a fixed path representative, has slope zero or one in the varying length. Exactly one edge has slope one. At zero the unique collapsed competitor pair has endpoint product minus one. Positivity forces the target's representative to be the single varying edge numerically, and its endpoint product identifies a trivalent pendant edge. No assertion about preservation of strict ordering at zero is needed.
4. Separating the constant and varying exponential terms gives equality of N and H. Deleting the leaf and changing the neighbor's defect from one to zero subtracts all of H except its unit contribution from the marked vertex. Thus the pair measure becomes N−H+1 and the signed marked profile H−1. Suppression of a degree-two point preserves both. The two removed vertices each contributed one to the square budget and the reduction preserves excess on both sides.
5. In an isometric pruned competitor the marked profile has the same number of atoms as the target has vertices. The competitor must realize all distances separately. For the target, replacing the two split edge lengths by their sum and one endpoint distance is an invertible rational coordinate change. Same-side sums of two distances from the point have a nonzero marked-coordinate coefficient. Opposite-side sums are actual paths, with the unique single-edge sum given by the endpoints of the containing edge. Distinct reduced edge lengths therefore identify this edge intrinsically. A reflection changes the distance to the far endpoint of any incident edge. Matching it on the opposite side would require that same edge to lie there. This excludes reflection even when the marked edge is pendant. For a one-edge tree reflection genuinely extends to an isometry. Consequently regrafting a common leaf preserves isometry exactly as required.
6. The pruned nonisomorphic real pair determines a rational ordering cell, and rational independence of its remaining target lengths forces full projection dimension. Iterating until at most 6Δ leaves gives at most 12Δ−2 reduced vertices. The full generic-identifiability failure, not merely one isolated real ambiguity, descends.
