# Laborde–Payan–Xuong conjecture

**Partial research.** Two sufficient conditions give independent transversals of all longest directed paths. The first uses the strongly connected components of the asymmetric arcs lying on more than one directed cycle. The second reduces a traceable substitution to maximum-weight supports of capacitated walks and proves the conjectured conclusion when the quotient components admit endpoint-preserving spanning walks within the capacities. Both conditions give transversals consisting of possible first vertices of longest paths. The unrestricted conjecture remains open. [Tracking issue #370](https://github.com/1337isnot1337/solved-conjectures/issues/370)

## Target and sources

Laborde, Payan, and Xuong ask whether every finite loopless digraph has an independent set meeting all its longest directed paths. Their stronger version requires each selected vertex to start some longest path. A path has no repeated vertices, and independent means there is no arc in either direction between two selected vertices. The question is the first case of the directed path partition conjecture and would imply Gallai–Roy by iteration. The November 24, 2026 source check found no unrestricted resolution. Havet proves the case of independence number at most two. Frick and coauthors give early criteria based on path starts. Benítez-Bobadilla and coauthors handle digraphs with suitable long-ear decompositions. [Original 1983 citation and both statements](https://dwest.web.illinois.edu/regs/lapaxuo.html) · [Havet (2004)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.disc.2004.07.013) · [Frick et al., §2](https://bibliotekanauki.pl/articles/744375.pdf) · [Benítez-Bobadilla et al. (2025), Theorem 4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.01918)

## 1. Complete the reverse arcs certified by longest paths

Let $D$ be a digraph, $L$ the number of vertices in a longest path, and $S$ the set of first vertices of such paths. For distinct $x,y\in S$, call $x$ mandatory for $y$ if every $L$-vertex path starting at $y$ contains $x$. Whenever $x\to y$ is an arc in $D[S]$, $x$ is mandatory for $y$, since otherwise the arc prepends $x$ to a longest path. This observation underlies Frick et al.'s detour-source kernel criterion. [Frick et al., Theorem 2.3](https://bibliotekanauki.pl/articles/744375.pdf)

Define $J$ on $S$ with the same underlying adjacency as $D[S]$. At each adjacent pair $x,y$, include $x\to y$ precisely when $x$ is mandatory for $y$. In particular every original arc survives. If one direction only survives, it is an original asymmetric arc and some longest path starting at its tail omits its head.

An independent out-dominating set in $J$ is independent in $D$, because the adjacent pairs of $J$ and $D[S]$ agree. For any longest path in $D$ with start $y$, either $y$ is in the set or a selected $x$ sends an arc to $y$ in $J$ and is mandatory for it. Thus such a set gives the stronger conclusion. Out-dominating refers to a kernel of the arc-reversed digraph, with the convention that a kernel is an independent set to which every outside vertex sends an arc.

**Theorem 1.** Let $R$ be the spanning subdigraph of $D$ consisting of its asymmetric arcs which lie in at least two distinct directed cycles of $D$. If for each strongly connected component $C$ of $R$ the induced underlying graph $D[V(C)]$ is bipartite, then $D$ has an independent transversal of its longest paths contained in $S$.

**Proof.** Write $J_{m a}$ for the asymmetric arcs of $J$. Consider a directed cycle $C$ of $J_{m a}$. Its arcs are all original, asymmetric arcs of $D$. For a vertex $v$ on $C$, let $v^-$ and $v^+$ be its predecessor and successor. Since $v\to v^+$ is asymmetric in $J$, there is a longest $v$-starting path which avoids $v^+$. It contains $v^-$ by the mandatory predecessor observation. The segment from $v$ to $v^-$ followed by $v^-\to v$ is a directed cycle in $D$ through $v^-\to v$ which omits $v^+$. Thus every arc of $C$ lies in at least two directed cycles of $D$. This cycle-overlap argument is the one used in Frick et al.'s proof of Corollary 2.2.

Every arc internal to a strongly connected component of $J_{m a}$ is on a directed cycle of $J_{m a}$. Hence each such component is contained in a component of $R$. Its induced adjacency in $J$ is bipartite by assumption. The asymmetric-component kernel criterion of Galeana-Sánchez and Neumann-Lara gives a kernel in the reverse of $J$, which is the required independent out-dominating set. [Frick et al., Corollary 2.2](https://bibliotekanauki.pl/articles/744375.pdf) · [Galeana-Sánchez–Neumann-Lara, Theorem 2.5 and Corollary 2.4](https://www.matem.unam.mx/~ilan/2013/1/seminario/onkernelperfect.pdf)

For completeness, the bipartite instance of that kernel criterion follows by choosing a source component of the asymmetric part. Its induced subdigraph has no odd cycle, so it has an independent out-dominating set by Richardson. Delete that set and its out-neighbors. Any remaining vertex adjacent to the chosen set would have to send an asymmetric arc into the source component, which is impossible. The remaining asymmetric components refine the ones before deletion and have the same bipartite property. Repeat and take the union of the chosen sets. This produces an independent out-dominating set in the whole digraph. ∎

The hypothesis allows arbitrary overlap among directed cycles in a bipartite part. For example, start with a strongly connected bipartite digraph and attach directed odd cycles at individual vertices, with the new cycles otherwise disjoint from the original digraph and each other. Every newly attached arc is in just one directed cycle. The components of $R$ stay in the original bipartite part, regardless of the cycle overlap there. A stronger structural consequence of the proof is that a counterexample to the unrestricted conjecture must have some non-bipartite subset of longest-path starts on which the asymmetric arcs of $J$ are strongly connected. Indeed the kernel criterion forces at least one non-bipartite component of $J_{\rm a}$.

## 2. Traceable substitutions and capacitated quotient walks

Let $Q$ be a loopless digraph. For each of its vertices $i$, take a disjoint nonempty digraph $H_i$ with a Hamiltonian directed path. Replace each arc $i\to j$ of $Q$ by all arcs from $H_i$ to $H_j$. Put $m_i=|V(H_i)|$. A walk in $Q$ is feasible if it visits $i$ at most $m_i$ times, counting each occurrence separately. Its support weight is $\sum_{i\text{ visited}}m_i$.

**Theorem 2.** The sets of modules visited by longest paths in the substitution are exactly the supports of feasible walks of maximum support weight. Every such longest path uses all vertices in each module it visits. The substitution has an independent transversal of its longest paths exactly when $Q$ has an independent set meeting all the maximum-weight supports. The versions requiring chosen vertices to be possible starts also correspond. On the quotient side the requirement is that each chosen module starts at least one maximum-weight feasible walk.

**Proof.** Compress consecutive runs of an actual directed path in a module to one occurrence of its quotient vertex. This gives a feasible walk. The actual path has size at most the support weight of the walk. Conversely for a feasible walk, split a fixed Hamiltonian path of each visited $H_i$ into as many nonempty consecutive segments as the number of occurrences of $i$. Use these segments in sequence at those occurrences. The intermodule arcs exist by construction. This lifts the walk to a path with exactly the support weight, using all vertices in every visited module. These constructions give both claims about optimal paths. If a walk starts in module $i$, its lift can start at the chosen first vertex of a fixed Hamiltonian path of $H_i$.

An independent set in the substitution projects to an independent quotient set because intermodule arcs are complete wherever they occur. Conversely, one representative in each module of an independent quotient set is independent. Because every longest lifted path saturates every visited module, the representatives meet every longest path exactly when their quotient set meets every optimal support. The same equivalence holds for admissible starts using the first vertex of the fixed Hamiltonian path in each selected module. ∎

**Corollary.** Suppose that for each strongly connected component of $Q$ and each ordered pair of its vertices $a,b$, there is a feasible walk in that component from $a$ to $b$ which visits all the vertices of the component. Then the substitution has an independent transversal consisting of possible starts. This hypothesis holds if each $m_i$ is at least the size of its quotient component. It also holds if each nontrivial quotient component has a Hamiltonian cycle and each $m_i\ge2$ there.

To prove the corollary, a quotient walk encounters each strongly connected component in one consecutive portion. Fix the entry and exit vertices of that portion. If a maximum-weight walk omits any vertex of the component, replace the portion by a feasible spanning walk with those endpoints. This strictly raises the weight and preserves feasibility elsewhere, a contradiction. Therefore every longest path uses all vertices of the corresponding substitution components it visits.

Consider all quotient components in which maximum-weight walks can start. There is no arc between two of them. If there were an arc from the first component to the second, take a maximum-weight walk starting in the second. Change its first portion to start at the head of this arc and keep its exit unchanged. Then prepend the tail of the arc, in the first component, which has not already been visited since the component digraph is acyclic. This raises the support weight. It follows that one can pick a module in each starting component without creating any adjacency between the chosen modules. Each such module can start a maximum-weight walk by the same rerouting, and their union meets every maximum-weight support. Theorem 2 gives the required transversal.

For a component of size $r$, visit all its vertices in an order beginning at $a$ and ending at $b$, repeating $a$ at the end if $b=a$. Connect successive vertices by simple directed paths. There are at most $r$ segments, so each vertex occurs at most $r$ times after joining the segments. For a nontrivial component with a Hamiltonian cycle and capacities at least two, go around its cycle once from $a$ and continue to $b$ if needed. Each vertex occurs at most twice. The case of one vertex uses its singleton walk. The ability to reroute both endpoints with repeated quotient visits extends the simpler case of a Hamiltonian component at a detour's start, recorded by van Aardt, Frick, and Singleton. [van Aardt–Frick–Singleton, Lemma 12](https://doi.org/10.7151/dmgt.1650)

The kernel construction does not establish that $J$ always has a kernel, and the walk reduction leaves arbitrary capacities open. Priority for the stated extensions is provisional.
