# Chen–Chvátal conjecture

**Partial research.** A smallest counterexample has no metric cut point. The proof gives an exact line-counting formula and a reduction to smaller metric spaces. The ordinary line-incidence matrix can still be singular without a universal line, even at aspect ratio two. Its nullity can grow linearly with the order at bounded aspect ratio. [Tracking issue #304](https://github.com/1337isnot1337/solved-conjectures/issues/304)

The target is that every finite metric space on at least two points has a universal line or at least as many distinct lines as points. For distinct $u,v$, the line $L(u,v)$ consists of both endpoints and those points forming a tight triangle with them. The November 24, 2026 primary-source screen located no general resolution. Aboulker and Kapadia exclude cut vertices in their argument for distance-hereditary graph metrics. The reduction here uses only metric equalities across a cut point, with an exact count. The bounded search did not locate the general formula or the rank examples. Priority is provisional. [Chen and Chvátal](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dam.2007.05.036) · [Aboulker and Kapadia, Claim 1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejc.2014.06.009) · [Huang, general lower bound](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.15058) · [2026 primary status](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dam.2025.10.044)

## 1. A cut-point reduction

Suppose $X$ is the union of $t\ge2$ sets $X_i$, any two meeting in the point $o$, with each $X_i$ containing another point. Write $r_u=d(o,u)$. Assume that $d(u,v)=r_u+r_v$ whenever $u,v$ are nonroot points in different sets. For $u\in X_i\setminus\{o\}$, put

```math
R_i(u)=\{w\in X_i:d(u,w)=|r_u-r_w|\}.
```

This includes $o$ and $u$. Write $h_i$ for the number of distinct sets $R_i(u)$ and $l_i$ for the number of distinct lines in the full space generated by pairs inside $X_i$, including pairs using $o$.

**Theorem.** If $X$ has no universal line, then

```math
\ell(X)=\sum_i l_i+\sum_{i<j} h_i h_j.
```

Choose any $y_i\in X\setminus X_i$ and let $Y_i=(X_i\setminus\{o\})\cup\{y_i\}$. Every $Y_i$ has no universal line, and

```math
\ell(X)\ge\sum_i \ell(Y_i).
```

In particular if all the $Y_i$ satisfy the conjecture, $\ell(X)\ge |X|+t-1$. Thus a smallest counterexample has no such cut point.

**Proof.** A pair inside $X_i$ includes all points outside $X_i$ on its line exactly when $d(u,v)=|r_u-r_v|$, and otherwise includes none. For a nonroot pair in different $X_i,X_j$, the line is $R_i(u)\cup R_j(v)$. These facts follow by substituting the cross distances in the three triangle equalities. For three nonroot points in different sets, each potential equality is strict because the right-hand side adds twice the radius of the middle point.

Every $R_i(u)$ is proper in $X_i$. Otherwise $L(o,u)$ would include all of $X_i$ and all outside points. In fact $h_i\ge2$. For any nonroot $u$, the line $L(o,u)$ extends outside $X_i$, so there is $w$ in $X_i$ not on it. Then $w$ is not in $R_i(u)$ and is in $R_i(w)$.

No two lines generated within different sets coincide. If both extend outside their sets, coincidence would make both universal. If one stays in its own set, a line generated in another set contains a nonroot point outside the first. The lines generated across sets are distinguished by their pair of sets and by their two radial sets. They are also distinct from lines generated within one set, since their traces in their two sets are proper and contain nonroot points. This proves the exact count.

For a pair within $X_i\setminus\{o\}$, its line in $Y_i$ is the restriction of its full line. If it contains $y_i$, it extends outside $X_i$ in the full space and also contains $o$. So a universal line of this type in $Y_i$ would be universal in $X$. The lines of $Y_i$ involving $y_i$ have traces $R_i(u)\setminus\{o\}$ on the other points and are proper. Thus $\ell(Y_i)\le l_i+h_i$. Finally $\sum_{i<j} h_i h_j\ge\sum_i h_i$ for $t\ge2$ and all $h_i\ge2$. Each term is at least $h_i+h_j$ since $(h_i-1)(h_j-1)\ge1$. This proves the claimed inequality. Since $|Y_i|=|X_i|\lt |X|$, a smallest counterexample cannot have the cut point. $\square$

Equivalently, in a smallest counterexample, for every $o$ the graph on $X\setminus\{o\}$ with edges $uv$ satisfying $d(u,v)\lt d(u,o)+d(o,v)$ must be connected.

## 2. What line-incidence rank can detect

Write the line-incidence matrix with a row per line and one column per point, over $\mathbb R$. Full column rank would imply the line count. The following classification identifies the smallest possible support of a kernel vector when there is no universal line.

**Proposition.** A nonzero kernel vector has support at least four. If its support has size four, its values are a common nonzero magnitude with two positive and two negative signs. Every triple of the four support points is collinear. Each outside point is collinear with either every pair of the support or exactly the pairs of a perfect matching between the two signs. Both perfect matchings between the signs occur among outside points.

**Proof.** A line meeting the support must meet it in a nonempty zero-sum subset. Suppose the support has only one point of either sign. Then every line through any point of the opposite sign must contain the unique point. All triples with that fixed pair are collinear, so the line generated by the pair is universal. A support with values of only one sign is impossible. This proves the size bound and shows that a support of size four splits two and two.

The two values on each side sum to the same magnitude, because the line of the same-sign pair with larger total must cancel it using at most the full opposite total. Therefore the two same-sign pairs each have both opposite points on their lines. All four triples are collinear. No three-element subset of this support sums to zero. If a point has at most one opposite point of equal magnitude, then either every line it generates contains all four, or every such line contains that one opposite point. Both give a universal pair. Thus all magnitudes coincide.

For an outside point $z$, join two support points if they are collinear with $z$. At each support vertex its closed neighbourhood must have signed sum zero, by applying the kernel condition to its line with $z$. A same-sign edge forces all four cross-sign edges, and then the other same-sign edge, so the graph is complete. Without a same-sign edge each vertex has one opposite neighbour, so the graph is one of the two perfect matchings. If one of the matchings never occurs, an edge from the other is collinear with every outside point and generates a universal line. $\square$

### Sharp example and unbounded nullity

Use the four-cycle $0,1,2,3$ with adjacent distance $2$ and opposite distance $4$. Add $z,w$ with distance vectors to the cycle $(2,4,4,2)$ and $(2,2,4,4)$ respectively, and set $d(z,w)=3$. This is a metric since every positive distance lies in $[2,4]$. It has eight lines and none is universal. The vector $(1,-1,1,-1,0,0)$ is in its kernel. The two matchings of the proposition are supplied by $z,w$. The support proposition requires two outside points, so this realizes a four-point dependency with the minimum possible total of six points.

Make $t$ copies of the example and identify their $z$ points, taking this common point to be $o$. Use the cut-point distance across copies. The order is $5t+1$ and the aspect ratio is at most four. Inside each copy the root never lies between two nonroot points. The three radial sets at $o$ have nonroot parts $\{0,1\}$, $\{2,3\}$, and $\{w\}$. Each has signed sum zero under the displayed vector. A line generated in one copy contains every or no nonroot point of each other copy, so all $t$ copies of the vector extend independently to the full kernel. The same radial sets show that no line generated across copies is universal, and no one-copy generator is universal even within its own copy.

The nullity is exactly $t$. For $t\ge2$, varying the radial set on one side of a cross line shows that the three radial nonroot parts have a common sum in their copy. The four-cycle line has no nonroot point from other copies and is the union of the first two parts, so their common sum is zero. Cross lines then force the shared root weight to be zero. The lines generated from $w$ to the cycle give the other pairings $\{1,2\}$ and $\{3,0\}$, each with zero sum, leaving precisely the alternating vector on each copy. For $t=1$, the lines consisting of the cycle alone and the cycle with either $z$ or $w$ give zero weights at both extra points, and the same pair equations apply.

These examples obstruct a proof by full incidence rank. The cut-point reduction concerns the number of distinct lines and leaves the unrestricted conjecture open.
