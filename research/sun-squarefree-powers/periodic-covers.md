# When a congruence cover would disprove Sun’s conjecture

Let $S$ be the set of squarefree integers of the form $2^a+5^b$, with $a\ge1$ and $b\ge0$. The [parity reduction](README.md) asks for an odd prime of the form $n-ms$, with $s\in S$, where $m=1$ for even $n$ and $m=2$ for odd $n$. Sun offers US$250 for the first explicit counterexample. [Original statement and terms](https://oeis.org/A304081)

## A cover would yield counterexamples of both parities

Fix an odd squarefree modulus $M$, and assign a residue $r$ modulo $M$. Consider the even targets congruent to $r$ and the odd targets congruent to $2r$ modulo $M$. Exactly one of the following holds.

- Some $s\in S$ satisfies $\gcd(r-s,M)=1$. In each progression this fixed shift supplies infinitely many represented targets. Indeed, the desired odd primes run through a reduced class modulo $2M$, so Dirichlet’s theorem applies. For each fixed $M$, the prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions gives order $X/\log X$ such targets up to $X$ as $X$ tends to infinity.
- Every $s\in S$ satisfies $\gcd(r-s,M)>1$. In each progression all but $O_M((\log X)^2)$ targets up to $X$ are counterexamples. Any prime residual must divide $M$, because multiplication by two is invertible modulo $M$. For each prime divisor, there are at most $O((\log X)^2)$ pairs of exponents that could produce a positive residual up to $X$. Each pair gives at most one target for that prime and parity.

Thus a complete finite cover for either parity would yield counterexamples of both parities. The second alternative gives an effective search: enumerate increasing members of the progression and test only the finitely many prime divisors of $M$ as possible residuals. The covering hypothesis ensures termination with a counterexample. An isolated counterexample could also occur in the first alternative.

## Which primes can a common period use?

If $p\ne2,5$ and the two power sequences separately repeat modulo $p$ with a common period $L$, then $p$ divides $G_L=\gcd(2^L-1,5^L-1)$. Conversely each prime divisor of $G_L$ has this periodicity. We allow 5 as an additional prime in every case, including the boundary exponent $b=0$. The prime 2 cannot divide an admissible residual.

**Finite obstruction theorem.** For every $1\le L\le2520$ and every assignment of one forbidden residue modulo each prime dividing $5G_L$, there exists a genuinely squarefree $s\in S$ below one trillion avoiding all those residues. This also holds for every subset of these primes. It follows that *every* progression built from those primes falls under the first alternative above. The endpoint 2520 is the least common multiple of the integers from 1 through 10. The calculation also tests periods below it that do not divide it.

The [standard-library checker](periodic_covers.py) generates all 545 distinct squarefree shifts at most $10^{12}-3$ by sieving primes and testing their squares. For each period it obtains the primes of $G_L$ by complete trial division. After redundant subsets are removed there are 468 maximal sets of primes.

At a search node, let $T$ be the surviving shifts. Each remaining prime can remove at most the size of its largest residue class in $T$. If the sum of those capacities is less than $|T|$, at least one shift survives every choice. Otherwise take the prime of largest capacity and recurse over all its distinct effects on $T$, including an empty class whenever applicable. At a leaf with no primes, the capacity sum is zero. This proves the check by induction. The complete run visited 468, 324, 455, 756, 1,000 and 25 nodes at depths zero through five. Python 3.10 or newer suffices.

## An obstruction for arbitrarily large odd periods

There is also a symbolic obstruction for certificates which assign a prime divisor to each residual or a square divisor to each inadmissible shift, with the divisibility tests periodic in the exponents. The following sufficient condition applies to every odd prime period and infinitely many composite periods:

```math
L>1\text{ odd},\qquad
\sum_{d\mid L,\ d>1}\frac{\log 2}{\log(2d+1)}<1.
```

In fact, assume only that the order of 2 modulo each odd testing prime other than 5 divides $L$. Fix $b=0$. For every such prime $q$, the subgroup generated by 2 has odd order and so excludes $-1$. Therefore $q$ cannot divide the shift $2^a+1$, even once. For a residual test at $p$, the congruence $m(2^a+1)\equiv n\pmod p$ rules out at most one residue of $a$ modulo $d=\mathrm{ord}_p(2)$. Here $d>1$. For each $d\mid L$, all primes of exact order $d$ divide $2^d-1$ and are at least $2d+1$, since $d$ is odd and divides $p-1$. Their number is at most $\log(2^d-1)/\log(2d+1)$. The union bound over $a\bmod L$ gives the displayed sufficient condition. At least one class of $a$ survives.

Independently choose $a\bmod4$ so that both $2^a+1$ and $n-m(2^a+1)$ are nonzero modulo 5. At most two of the four possible values of $2^a\bmod5$ are excluded. Since $L$ is odd, the Chinese remainder theorem gives a positive $a$ meeting both choices. The shift is odd, and the residual has odd parity. Thus neither 2 nor 5 can complete such a cover. For example, an odd prime $L$ has just one summand in the bound. The same argument applies to $L=uv$ for distinct primes $u,v\ge7$, since there are three summands, each smaller than one third.

This last argument rules out a certificate using the specified periodic tests. A prime outside the tests can still divide the shift to the second power. The finite obstruction theorem above avoids that limitation in its stated range by using shifts whose squarefreeness was checked outright. Neither obstruction decides whether an individual large target has a prime representation.
