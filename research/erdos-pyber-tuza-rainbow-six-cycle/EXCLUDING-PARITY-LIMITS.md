# Erdős–Pyber–Tuza rainbow six-cycle conjecture

**No sequence of rainbow-free, exactly balanced six-colourings on $6n+1$ vertices can converge to any parity graphon.** This removes all support hypotheses from the earlier parity-limit arguments. The graphons here can be weighted, nonhomogeneous and arbitrarily sparse in a permissible individual colour, subject only to balance and exact parity. The remaining limit problem is to show that every balanced graphon with zero rainbow six-cycle density is a parity graphon.

In a parity graphon the vertices are split into two halves, three colours occur only within halves, and the other three only across them. Each colour has degree $1/6$ at almost every vertex. Normalizing the measure of either half to one gives a symmetric three-colour graphon with each colour of degree $1/3$ and the three kernels summing to one.

## A three-colour kernel identity

Let $A_1,A_2,A_3$ be the integral operators of a symmetric balanced three-colour graphon on a probability space. Write $J$ for the constant-one kernel. Thus $A_1+A_2+A_3=J$, each $A_i J=J A_i=J/3$, and $J^2=J$. Composition need not be commutative. The kernel that counts ordered three-edge paths using all three colours is
```math
\sum_{\pi\in S_3} A_{\pi(1)}A_{\pi(2)}A_{\pi(3)}
=2\sum_{i=1}^3 A_i^3.
```
To see this, start with the sum of all ordered products, equal to $J$. Subtract the products counted by each of the three possible equal-index pairs. Each such sum is $J/3$, including the nonadjacent equality, whose sum is $\sum_i A_iJA_i$. The all-equal terms have been subtracted three times instead of once; add back twice their sum.

For every nonnegative symmetric bounded kernel $W$, its three-step kernel $W^3$ is positive at almost every pair where $W$ is positive. Otherwise put $`H=W\mathbf 1_{\{W^3=0\}}`$, a nonzero symmetric nonnegative kernel. Since $H\le W$, one would have $\int H H^3=0$. But this integral is $t(C_4,H)=\int (H^2)^2>0$: the symmetric $H^2$ has positive integral as the squared degrees of $H$ have positive integral. This is a contradiction. Since the three colour kernels sum to one, the three-colour path kernel in the displayed identity is **positive almost everywhere**. Thus for every fixed $\eta>0$, its integral between any two sets of measure at least $\eta$ is bounded below by a uniform positive constant. Indeed, the measure where the kernel is below a positive threshold tends to zero with the threshold.

## Moving from a weighted limit to a finite core

Suppose a sequence of balanced finite colourings with $n\to\infty$ converges in coloured cut metric to a parity graphon. Relabel to realize cut convergence on a common probability space. Round the two limit halves to a vertex partition by assigning each vertex atom to its majority half. The resulting two parts differ from the halves by total measure $o(1)$: the sum of the crossing-colour kernels converges in $L^1$ to the zero-one crossing indicator, by summing its signed cut errors on the four half-blocks. On a straddling vertex atom the two target rows differ everywhere, so its integrated error is at least the mass of the smaller half. This proves the rounding claim. It also gives $o(n^2)$ edges of incorrect parity in the rounded partition, and each part has $3n+o(n)$ vertices.

Fix a constant $\eta>0$ sufficiently small, for example $1/1000$. The kernel identity on either original half gives a uniform positive normalized density of paths using the three within-half colours between any two endpoint pools of at least $\eta n$ vertices in the same rounded part. To justify the transfer, consider the integral of each coloured three-edge path with its endpoint variables restricted to the pools and all four path variables restricted to that half. Replace the three finite kernels by the limiting ones one at a time. For any replaced path edge, fixing the remaining variables gives a product of separate bounded tests in the two edge variables; its integral error is bounded by the cut norm. Replacing the rounded half restriction by the original half restriction costs $o(1)$ per variable. Thus the transfer is uniform in the endpoint pools. The identity gives a positive bound even after extending the finite-colouring exclusions to omit any two named vertices and all paths with repeated vertices, since these discard only $O(n^3)$ of the $\Omega(n^4)$ ordered tuples.

Call a vertex typical if it is incident with fewer than $\eta n$ incorrect-parity edges. Only $o(n)$ vertices are atypical. For a vertex $u$ and a crossing hue $a$, a side is available if $u$ has at least $2\eta n$ neighbours of that hue in the side. Each colour has degree $n$, so some side is available. Record the side bit *flipped*, reflecting the nominal crossing parity; let $Q(u,a)$ be the nonempty set of these recorded bits. A typical vertex has exactly one available side in every crossing hue, and its recorded bit there is its own rounded side.

## The three crossing hues force a forbidden exact cut

Take any edge $uv$ of crossing hue $c$, and let $a,b$ be the other two crossing hues. If $Q(u,a)$ and $Q(v,b)$ contained the same bit, then the available pools would lie in the same rounded side. The transferred three-colour path property joins those pools inside that side, using the three distinct within-half colours and using neither $u$ nor $v$. Adding the two terminal edges of hues $a,b$ and the edge $uv$ would make a rainbow six-cycle. Consequently in a rainbow-free example every bit of $Q(u,a)$ is opposite every bit of $Q(v,b)$, and the same holds with $a,b$ exchanged.

For any $u$ and hue $c$, there is a colour-$`c`$ neighbour $v$ that is typical, since $n$ exceeds the number of atypical vertices. Both of its terminal hue sets have the same singleton bit. The two constraints therefore show that the two terminal hue sets at $u$ are also equal singletons. Vary $c$; all three recorded bits at $u$ are equal, say $q_u$. Every edge of any of the crossing hues joins opposite $q$-classes. Their union is a bipartite graph of degree $3n$ at every vertex, so its two parts must have the same size, impossible on $6n+1$ vertices.

This rules out every parity limit, including those with holes in the support of all six permitted hues. The conclusion does not assume that a non-parity zero-density limit is impossible. By compactness, finding any counterexample sequence now requires a **genuinely non-parity** balanced zero-density graphon.
