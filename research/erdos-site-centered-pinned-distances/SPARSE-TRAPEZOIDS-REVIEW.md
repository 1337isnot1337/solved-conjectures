# Independent review of the two sparse-trapezoid implications

A separate Codex reviewer in WadeDavis’s research session reviewed the proof on 27 November 2026 UTC. The two conclusions pass as written. In both, each positive-radius circle centered at a site contains at most three sites. The first conclusion assumes a sufficiently small absolute upper bound on the number of counted trapezoids divided by the square of the set size. The second allows any fixed finite upper bound for that ratio on every subset. The conclusions concern the mean number of pinned positive distances. The unrestricted site-centered problem remains open. [Original question, p. 168](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1987-27.pdf#page=2)

## Trapezoids, carriers and uniform local estimates

For a fixed perpendicular bisector, different reflected pairs are disjoint orbits of the reflection. Two on different carriers have distinct parallel joining lines. They give four distinct sites and specify a pairing of those sites. A four-site set has at most three such pairings. This handles the two axes of an ordinary rectangle and the excluded collinear pairs. With carrier multiplicities $`m_i`$, total $`u`$ and maximum $`h`$, the quantity $`u^2-\sum_i m_i^2`$ counts twice the cross-carrier pairs and bounds $`(u-h)^2`$ from above. Therefore the squared unchosen multiplicities sum to at most $`6Q(S)`$ for any base set $`S`$.

Every two pins determine at most one axis. Cauchy–Schwarz bounds the unchosen contribution from axes having at least two pins by a constant times $`p\sqrt{Q(S)}`$. Axes with one pin contribute at most the number of base pairs. Under $`Q(P)=o(n^2)`$, this gives the required $`O(s^2)+o(n^2)`$ uniformly. Failure of every absolute choice in the first assertion would produce just such a sequence, by successively taking both positive constants at most $`1/j`$ and requiring the set size to exceed $`j`$.

For the hereditary assertion, the dyadic rich-line estimate gives squared pin richness $`O(p^2/K+pM)`$ above $`K`$, where $`M`$ is the largest ambient line occupancy. Apply $`Q(S)\le Cs^2`$ before taking the square root. Rich unchosen incidences cost $`O_C(sp/\sqrt K+s\sqrt{pM})`$ and the remaining axes cost $`O(Ks^2)`$. The given choice of $`K`$ balances the first and last terms when $`p\ge s`$. For $`p<s`$ both are $`O_C(s^2)`$. The error divided by $`n^2`$ is at most a constant times $`\sqrt{M/n}`$, which tends to zero. Each incidence extends to at most one third base by the site-centered hypothesis. The chosen-pair estimate needs only disjoint pairs on the single chosen carrier of an axis. Its weighted dyadic count and the intersection argument for two large triangle sides therefore agree with the earlier proof.

## Inheritance and the order of limits

The near-equality identity for full radius classes gives asymptotic uniform product law for the pin and each single base. The mixed-line and mixed-circle theorems imply sublinear occupancy of every line and circle uniformly on the sequence. Consequently a fixed independent sample has neither collinear triples nor cocircular quadruples with probability tending to one. In the farthest-site estimate there are finitely many pieces at fixed sample size, and the uniform error tends to zero before the sample size grows. In the tightness estimate the two powers of escaped mass both exceed one. Taking the retained fraction close to one gives the same contradiction, with constants depending on the fixed hereditary bound when relevant.

A positive-mass child is selected using a shrinking ball and a concentric ball of three times its radius with the same limiting mass. The mates of an internal good pair in the smaller ball lie in the larger, and each mate’s marginal bounds the probability of its lying in the intervening annulus by a vanishing amount. Thus the child inherits the near-product law. A fixed node of relative size $`\alpha>0`$ inherits $`Q=o(n_{\rm node}^2)`$ in the first case, and all subsets retain the same constant in the second. Countable diagonal extraction suffices because each subsequent comparison uses a fixed positive-mass node.

## Marks in Appendices B and C

Choose one carrier per represented axis in the entire node before defining its target lines or midpoint sets. At that node rich unchosen incidences have normalized mass $`O_C(K^{-1/2})+o(1)`$, while the law for base pairs of bounded richness is dominated by a constant times the independent base-pair law. The latter gives zero measure to coinciding endpoints on a compact nonatomic graph portion, or to equal individual ranks under atomless one-rank marginals. Treat finite selection and matching as discrete flags in the joint subsequence.

In the terminal graph case first fix the spatial weight, its normalizing mass $`Z>0`$, and the desired error in the weighted law. Then take the unweighted error much smaller than $`Z`$ times that error. In the small-carrier bound choose the lower carrier cutoff first, then the richness threshold, and finally the positive coefficient in the $`n^{2/3}`$ cutoff. This order controls both the original chosen-pair bounds and the additional rich unchosen error. Intersections between two retained large lines have vanishing label mass, since each site marginal is asymptotically uniform. At a fixed pin, one specified target orientation has exactly one eligible carrier at most. Hence the discarded orientations and the perpendicular source–target line pairs obey their original bounds **for chosen sides**.

Mark a base when at least one side incident to it is chosen and both endpoints belong to the same retained assigned set. This rule is invariant under arbitrary permutations of the three bases. Once four graph coordinates force a coincidence of two bases, both labels of that side are marked except on the preceding errors. In the rank alternative a tie of a pin and base has probability zero. If no two labels tie, finite strict comparisons agree with their limits and the resulting slope bound prevents three equidistant bases. Both alternatives therefore give the marked-label probability used in Appendix C. Swapping the pin and a base is applied only to the weighted independent pin–site law, its assigned-set membership and the size comparison. Exchangeability of the three bases supplies the separate lower bound involving an actual mark. This distinction preserves the comparison after restricting the marks.

For Appendix B keep only the popular midpoints for which the target is the chosen carrier of the perpendicular bisector at that midpoint. The usual size bound on the midpoint set persists under restriction. Any marked contribution with a nonpopular midpoint has fewer than the prescribed number of representations. At a retained popular midpoint every counted target representation is available for the additive-energy calculation. For parallel targets their eligible midpoint preimages at any fixed source point are disjoint, by the one-carrier choice. This supplies exactly the orientation step needed after the small-doubling extraction. The common-coordinate count, projection-ratio injectivity and adjacent-ray bounds then contradict the lower bound on marked incidences. These checks cover both the weighted terminal argument and the unit-weight positive node on infinite paths. ## Scope and finite checks

The earlier two-sided condition bounds active completions after removing finitely many individual pairs per axis. Here the retained carrier can supply arbitrarily many pairs. The published bisector-energy statistic includes pairs on a common carrier. The present count permits them freely and also permits many cyclic quadruples without reflected pairs. The arbitrary fixed global condition $`Q(P)\le Cn^2`$ alone would not supply the factor $`s`$ in the hereditary local estimate. This proof gives no conclusion for that remaining case.
