# Barket et al.'s Cayley-diameter conjecture

**Status:** false as stated, with an infinite counterexample family and a corrected exhaustive classification for the undirected diameter used in the source. Unrefereed; no claim of exclusive priority.

Rashid Barket, Enrico Grimaldi, Yacoub Hendi, Edward Hirst, Adam Onus, and Harmeet Singh, [*Learning the Graphical Nature of Symmetries*](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.12026v1), Conjecture 4.2, assert that if
```math
D(\mathop{\mathrm{Cay}}(G,S))\ge |G|/d(G),
```
where $`d(G)`$ is the minimum size of a generating set of $`G`$, then $`G`$ is elementary abelian of order $`2^k`$ and $`|S|=k`$, for $`k=2,3,4`$. In the “Density and Diameter” definition in §4.2, $`D`$ is undirected diameter. The paper initially defines its Cayley graphs as directed, but the counterexample below has inverse-closed $`S`$, so it also refutes the implication if read using directed diameter.

## Counterexamples at every dihedral order

For any integer $`m\ge3`$, let
```math
G=D_{2m}=\langle r,t:r^m=t^2=1,\;trt=r^{-1}\rangle,\qquad S=\{t,rt\}.
```
Both elements of $`S`$ are involutions, and together they generate $`G`$ because $`(rt)t=r`$. The resulting graph is a connected simple 2-regular graph on $`2m`$ vertices, hence the cycle $`C_{2m}`$. Its diameter is $`m`$. The group is noncyclic, so $`d(G)=2`$, and
```math
D=m=\frac{|G|}{d(G)}.
```
But $`G`$ is not elementary abelian. Thus there are counterexamples of arbitrarily large order, even with a generating set of the minimum possible size. The identification of the cycle as a dihedral Cayley graph is classical; the use here is its application to the stated conjecture.

## Corrected classification

Let $`G`$ be a nontrivial finite group and $`S`$ any generating set not containing the identity. Use its underlying undirected Cayley graph. The inequality $`D\ge |G|/d(G)`$ holds exactly in the following cases:

1. $`G`$ is a dihedral group of order $`2m`$, $`m\ge2`$, and $`S`$ is a pair of generating involutions. Then $`D=m`$. For $`m=2`$, the group is $`C_2^2`$.
2. $`G=C_2^3`$ or $`C_2^4`$, and $`S`$ is a basis as a vector space over $`\mathbb F_2`$. Then $`D=3`$ or $`4`$, respectively.

Only the rank-three cube has strict inequality; all other cases listed give equality.

### Subgroup-chain bound

Write $`n=|G|`$, $`d=d(G)`$. Choose an inclusion-minimal generating subset $`T=\{s_1,\ldots,s_r\}`$ of $`S`$, and let $`G_i=\langle s_1,\ldots,s_i\rangle`$ with $`G_0=\{1\}`$. The chain is strict, since an $`s_i`$ generated by predecessors would be redundant in $`T`$. Put $`b_i=[G_i:G_{i-1}]\ge2`$. Then $`\prod_i b_i=n`$ and
```math
D(\mathop{\mathrm{Cay}}(G,S))\le D(\mathop{\mathrm{Cay}}(G,T))
  \le \sum_{i=1}^r(b_i-1)\le n/2^{r-1}+r-2. \tag{1}
```

For the middle inequality use the Schreier graph on the right cosets $`G_{i-1}x`$ in $`G_i`$. Its moves are right multiplication by $`s_1^{\pm1},\ldots,s_i^{\pm1}`$, well-defined on these cosets, and it is connected. From $`G_{i-1}`$ to any coset there is a walk of length at most $`b_i-1`$; following its labels gives a representative $`w`$ of that coset of word length at most $`b_i-1`$. If $`g`$ is in the target coset, write $`g=hw`$ with $`h\in G_{i-1}`$, then use induction for $`h`$. This bounds the word length from the identity, which is the diameter because the Cayley graph is vertex-transitive. For the last inequality of (1), replacing two factors $`(a,b)`$ with $`(2,ab/2)`$ preserves their product and does not decrease their sum when $`a,b\ge2`$, since $`(a-2)(b-2)\ge0`$. Apply it repeatedly to all but one factor. The intermediate factors need not be integers for this last numerical comparison.

### Ranks one and two

For $`d=1`$, the diameter is at most $`n-1<n`$. For $`d=2`$, necessarily $`n\ge4`$. Any finite connected vertex-transitive graph of order at least 3 has no cut vertex: a leaf of a spanning tree can be deleted while preserving connectivity, and automorphisms can send it to any vertex. Let $`L_i`$ be the distance-$`i`$ sphere about a vertex with a vertex at distance $`D`$ (Cayley graphs are vertex-transitive). Every intermediate sphere $`L_i`$, $`0<i<D`$, has at least two vertices, since one vertex in such a sphere would be a cut vertex. If $`D\ge 2`$ and the degree is at least three, then $`|L_1|\ge3`$, and
```math
n\ge 1+3+2(D-2)+1=2D+1.
```
Thus $`D\ge n/2`$ is only possible at degree two; $`D=1`$ also does not meet the bound because $`n\ge4`$. Degree one cannot occur for a connected graph of this order. In degree two the inverse closure of $`S`$ has two elements. If they are mutually inverse noninvolutions, they generate a cyclic group, contrary to $`d=2`$. Hence they are two involutions and $`S`$ contains both. A noncyclic group generated by involutions $`a,b`$ is dihedral: with $`r=ab`$ it has the normal forms $`r^i`$ and $`r^ia`$, and these sets are disjoint since $`a\in\langle r\rangle`$ would imply cyclicity. This proves exactly the first case.

### Ranks at least three

Here $`r\ge d\ge3`$ and $`n\ge2^r`$.

- If $`r\ge4`$, the difference

  $`\displaystyle \frac{n}{r}-\left(\frac{n}{2^{r-1}}+r-2\right)`$

  is increasing in $`n`$, and at $`n=2^r`$ equals $`2^r/r-r`$. It is nonnegative for $`r\ge4`$ and vanishes only for $`r=4,n=16`$, because $`2^r>r^2`$ for $`r\ge5`$ (induct from $`r=5`$). Therefore $`D<n/r\le n/d`$ except possibly when $`r=4,n=16`$. There $`d=4`$ is required: if $`d=3`$, (1) gives $`D\le4<16/3`$.
- If $`r=3`$, then $`d=3`$. If $`n>8`$, some $`b_i\ge3`$ so $`n\ge12`$. For $`n\ge12`$, (1) gives $`D\le n/4+1\le n/3`$, strictly if $`n>12`$. Every group of order 12 is 2-generated, as shown below, so only $`n=8`$ remains.

If $`n=2^d`$ with $`d=3`$ or $`4`$, then every nonidentity element has order 2. Otherwise start with the cyclic subgroup of an element of order at least 4, and add elements outside the subgroup they have generated until reaching $`G`$. Each addition multiplies the subgroup order by at least two, so at most $`d-2`$ additions after the starting element suffice, giving at most $`d-1`$ generators, a contradiction. A group of exponent 2 is abelian, so $`G=C_2^d`$. Any generating set contains a basis, whose Cayley graph is the $`d`$-cube. Any extra generator has Hamming weight at least two in that basis. The unique vector of weight $`d`$ then has word length at most $`1+d-2=d-1`$ (use the extra generator and the missing basis vectors), and every other vector already has word length at most $`d-1`$. Hence $`D=d`$ is attained exactly for a basis. For $`d=3,4`$, this proves the remaining claims.

For completeness, suppose $`|G|=12`$. If the Sylow 3-subgroup $`P`$ is unique, choose a Sylow 2-subgroup $`Q`$. Then $`G=PQ`$, and $`Q`$ either is cyclic (one generator for each factor suffices) or is $`C_2^2`$. In the latter case the action of $`Q`$ on $`P=C_3`$ has a nontrivial kernel because $`\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}(C_3)`$ has order two. Choose a nontrivial $`u`$ in the kernel, choose $`v\in Q\setminus\langle u\rangle`$, and let $`p`$ generate $`P`$. Since $`u`$ and $`p`$ commute and have coprime orders, $`pu`$ and $`v`$ generate the whole group. If there are four Sylow 3-subgroups, conjugation on them gives a transitive subgroup of $`S_4`$ and the image has order $`4`$ or $`12`$. Order $`4`$ would put all four Sylow 3-subgroups in the order-three kernel, impossible; thus $`G`$ embeds as the index-two subgroup $`A_4`$ of $`S_4`$. Here $`A_4`$ is 2-generated: two distinct 3-cycles with supports overlapping in two vertices generate a subgroup whose order is divisible by 3 and is transitive on four vertices, hence has order 12.

## If diameter is instead taken in the directed Cayley graph

The paper's explicit diameter definition is undirected. For completeness, the directed variant has exactly two additional types of rank-two generating pairs: for $`G=C_m\times C_2`$ with even $`m\ge4`$, write $`a`$ for a generator of $`C_m`$ and $`c`$ for the nonidentity element of $`C_2`$. The two types are $`S=\{a,c\}`$ and $`S=\{a,ac\}`$, up to group isomorphism. Each has directed diameter $`m`$. All the undirected qualifying cases qualify for the directed reading as well, and there are no others.

Indeed, the subgroup-chain bound (1) also applies to directed diameter. The Schreier graphs now use only positive generators, but remain strongly connected because in a finite group each generator's inverse is a positive power. Every target coset is reached by a shortest directed simple path of length at most $`b_i-1`$. The rank-one and rank-at-least-three arguments therefore still apply, and the latter exceptions have involutory generators.

For rank two, write $`n=2m`$ if it is even. An inclusion-minimal subset $`T`$ of size $`r\ge3`$ cannot attain $`D\ge n/2`$: by (1), $`D\le n/4+1<n/2`$, since $`n\ge2^r\ge8`$ and the right side of (1) is nonincreasing with $`r`$ on the allowed range. Thus $`T=\{a,b\}`$. In either order of this pair, the first subgroup is cyclic with order $`o(a)`$ or $`o(b)`$, and the numerical bound at $`r=2`$ is $`D\le n/2`$. Equality demands an index factor 2, in each ordering, so $`n=2m`$ and each of $`a,b`$ has order $`2`$ or $`m`$. Two involutions give the dihedral case; adding an extra generator to their bidirectional cycle immediately shortens the distance to its unique antipode, and no other distance can increase.

Otherwise choose $`a`$ of order $`m\ge3`$, so $`\langle a\rangle`$ has index two, and write $`bab^{-1}=a^s`$, $`b^2=a^k`$, with $`1\le s\le m-1`$ and $`0\le k\le m-1`$. All elements have representatives $`a^i`$ or $`a^ib`$ with $`0\le i<m`$; the only possible element of directed distance $`m`$ under $`T`$ is $`a^{m-1}b`$. If $`s\ge2`$, then $`a^{m-1-s}ba`$ reaches this element in $`m+1-s<m`$ steps. Hence $`s=1`$ and the group is abelian. If $`k\ge3`$, the word $`a^{m-1-k}b^3`$ also reaches the element in fewer than $`m`$ steps. The case $`k=1`$ makes $`G`$ cyclic, which is excluded, so $`k=0`$ or $`k=2`$. For $`k=0`$, $`b=c`$ is an independent involution; for $`k=2`$, $`c=a^{-1}b`$ is an independent involution. Thus $`G=C_m\times C_2`$ in either case, and its rank is two exactly when $`m`$ is even.

For $`T=\{a,c\}`$, in coordinates $`(i,j)\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}\times\{0,1\}`$ the distance is $`i+j`$, so the unique farthest vertex is $`(m-1,1)`$. Every extra generator has $`i+j\ge2`$ and permits reaching $`(m-1,1)`$ in at most $`1+(m-1-i)+(1-j)\le m-1`$ steps. For $`T=\{a,ac\}`$, the two generators both increment the first coordinate by one, and one toggles the second. Every vertex with first coordinate $`i\ge1`$ has distance $`i`$, and the unique farthest vertex is $`(0,1)`$, of distance $`m`$. Each extra generator is either $`(0,1)`$ or has first coordinate $`i\ge2`$. In the latter case it permits a path to $`(0,1)`$ of length at most $`1+m-i\le m-1`$. This proves the directed classification.

## Literature and scope

The original [arXiv record](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.12026) has one version, posted 13 July 2026. Exact-title, arXiv-ID, author, conjecture-number and dihedral-counterexample searches through 24 November 2026 found no earlier publication answering Conjecture 4.2 or giving the classification above. There is a separate prior counterexample to the source's Conjecture 4.4 (central-quotient eigengaps), not considered here. Some preceding remarks in the source discuss the computed sample, but the displayed conjecture does not specify a canonical selection of generating sets. The counterexample applies to the displayed statement for a chosen generating set and still uses a minimum-size set; this does not contradict claims limited to a particular implementation's choices. The additional broad correlation sentence in Conjecture 4.2 (“directly related to some group property”) does not specify a mathematical relationship, and is not addressed.
