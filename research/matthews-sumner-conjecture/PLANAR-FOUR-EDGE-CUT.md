# Matthews–Sumner conjecture

**Partial research.** A planar side of a cyclic four-edge cut can be replaced by a four-cycle when its four attachments are cofacial. Every dominating cycle of the smaller graph lifts to a dominating cycle of the original graph. Consequently a minimum counterexample has no such side with more than four vertices. [Tracking issue #502](https://github.com/1337isnot1337/solved-conjectures/issues/502)

## Target and sources

Matthews and Sumner conjectured that every 4-connected claw-free graph is Hamiltonian. The equivalent cubic formulation asks whether every cyclically 4-edge-connected cubic graph has a dominating cycle, meaning a cycle with at least one endpoint of every edge. The November 24, 2026 source screen found a recent reformulation using Tutte paths and no resolution. Broersma, Fijavž, Kaiser, Kužel, Ryjáček and Vrána establish the equivalence with the cubic formulation and give a general theory of compatible fragment replacements. The result below proves a specific compatibility in that framework for planar fragments of arbitrary order. The bounded search did not locate an earlier statement of this reduction. Priority is provisional. [Matthews and Sumner](https://doi.org/10.1002/jgt.3190080116) · [Broersma et al., Section 3 and Theorem 10](https://ris.utwente.nl/ws/files/6426370/sdarticle.pdf) · [2026 Tutte-path reformulation](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/jgt.70113)

## Reduction

Let $G$ be a connected simple cubic graph with no cut of size at most three separating two induced subgraphs that each contain a cycle. Suppose its vertex set is partitioned into $A$ and $B$, both inducing a subgraph with a cycle, with exactly four edges between them. Assume $G[A]$ has a plane drawing with its four attachment vertices on one face, in order $a,b,c,d$. Form $G_0$ by replacing $G[A]$ with the cycle $abcd$ and reattaching the four edges in the given order. Then $G_0$ is simple, cubic and cyclically 4-edge-connected. If $G_0$ has a dominating cycle, so does $G$.

### Connectivity

First, in any cubic graph that is cyclically 4-edge-connected, a cut separating two shores each containing an edge has size at least four. If both shores contain a cycle this is the definition. Otherwise one shore is a forest on $s\ge2$ vertices with $k\ge1$ components, and its cut has size $s+2k$.

At most one of the four crossing edges is incident with any given vertex of either shore. If a vertex has at least two such edges, it has at most one neighbor on its own shore. Moving it to the other shore preserves both cycles and leaves a cut of size at most three. Thus each shore has minimum internal degree two, and every component of either shore contains a cycle. Since every component needs at least four edges to the other shore, each shore is connected. Neither shore has a bridge. In fact deleting a bridge of one shore would split it into two components with at most one degree-one vertex each and no isolated vertices, hence each containing a cycle. Each would need at least four edges to its complement in $G$, including the bridge. The four original crossing edges cannot supply three more to each component.

These facts prove the connectivity assertion for $G_0$. A cut splitting both the new four-cycle and $B$ has at least two edges from each induced subgraph. A cut keeping the four-cycle on one shore lifts to $G$ with the same size, with a cyclic shore there in place of the four-cycle. A cut splitting the four-cycle while keeping all of $B$ on one shore has an acyclic shore.

### A planar line graph with four marked vertices

Adjoin a leaf and its edge at each attachment of $G[A]$. Write $L$ for the line graph of this graph with four leaves, and write $a,b,c,d$ also for the line-graph vertices representing the four leaf edges. $L$ is 2-connected. Deleting an internal-edge vertex corresponds to deleting one edge in the connected bridgeless graph $G[A]$. Deleting a leaf-edge vertex just discards that leaf edge. In both cases the remaining edges form one connected graph after isolated vertices are ignored.

Place the four leaves in the chosen face of $G[A]$. In the usual line-graph embedding, the edges incident to a vertex of degree three give a triangle, and the line-graph vertex of each leaf edge lies on the outside of its triangle. This embeds $L$ in the plane with the terminals on the outer cycle in order $a,b,c,d$. Put $J=L+\{ab,bc,cd,da\}$, adding the four edges in the outer face.

The graph $J$ is 4-connected. To see this, use the cyclically 4-edge-connected cubic graph $K$ obtained by replacing $B$ with a four-cycle with the same port order. The connectivity proof above applies with the shores exchanged. The line graph of $K$ is 4-connected, since deleting at most three edges of $K$ cannot leave two components that each contain an edge. Deleting the four vertices corresponding to the edges of the added cycle from that line graph leaves $L$. After deleting a set $S$ of at most three vertices of $J$, suppose first that the surviving terminals are connected along the added cycle of $J$. Then any walk between vertices of $L-S$ in the line graph of $K$ minus $S$ can have its excursions through the four auxiliary vertices replaced by walks in that surviving terminal cycle or path. Thus $J-S$ is connected. The only remaining case has exactly two opposite surviving terminals. Then $S$ removes at most one internal-edge vertex, and $G[A]$ is bridgeless, so $L-S$ is connected.

### The three possible local demands

We use two planar Tutte path theorems. In a 2-connected plane graph with outer cycle $X$, Thomassen gives an $X$-Tutte path between specified distinct vertices of $X$ through one prescribed edge of $X$. Every component off the path has at most three neighbors on it, and has at most two if its bridge contains an edge of $X$. Thomas and Yu, independently Sanders, give a Tutte path between $x$ and $y$ through two prescribed edges $e,f$ of $X$ when $x,y,e,f$ occur in that cyclic order. [Thomassen and Thomas–Yu path theorems, reproduced as (2.2) and (2.3)](https://yu.math.gatech.edu/Papers/pancyclic.pdf)

Whenever a Tutte path in $L$, or in $L$ plus some edges of the added cycle of $J$, contains all four terminals, the path spans that graph. Otherwise a component outside the path has at most three neighbors on it and adding the missing edges of $J$ leaves those neighbors unchanged. Removing the neighbors would disconnect $J$, since the path has at least four vertices.

**Adjacent endpoints.** For an adjacent terminal pair in the order $a,b,c,d$, take the outer-cycle arc between them that contains the other two terminals. Choose a distinct edge incident with each of those two terminals on the arc, in their order. The two-edge Tutte path theorem gives a path between the original pair through both edges. It contains all terminals, hence spans $L$.

**Opposite endpoints with a chosen third terminal.** Apply Thomassen’s theorem in $L$, prescribing an outer-cycle edge incident with the third terminal. Write $P$ for the resulting path and $u$ for the fourth terminal. Every component off $P$ that omits $u$ has at most three neighbors on $P$ and gains no neighbors when the four terminal edges of $J$ are added. By 4-connectivity, no such component exists if $P$ has at least four vertices. In fact $P$ does have at least four vertices, since its endpoints and the chosen third terminal are distinct, and no terminals are adjacent in $L$.

If a component $Q$ off $P$ contains $u$, its bridge contains an outer-cycle edge at $u$, so $Q$ has at most two neighbors on $P$. If $Q$ also contains a vertex representing an internal edge of $G[A]$, let $W$ be the set of endpoints in $A$ of all edges represented by vertices of $Q$. Then $W$ contains an edge of $G[A]$. Every internal edge of $G[A]$ leaving $W$ corresponds to a neighbor of $Q$ on $P$. The same is true for every attachment edge of $G$ leaving $W$ except possibly the one at $u$, by using its leaf-edge representative in $L$. Hence the cut of $W$ in $G$ has size at most three, contradicting the connectivity bound since $B$ contains an edge. Thus $P$ covers every vertex of $L$ except possibly $u$.

**Two noncrossing pairs.** For pairs $(a,b)$ and $(c,d)$, add just the edge $bc$ externally along the outer arc between $b,c$ with no other terminal. This edge and all four terminals are on the new outer cycle. A Tutte path from $a$ to $d$ through $bc$ contains all terminals and spans. Removing the prescribed edge leaves two disjoint paths. Their endpoint pairs are $(a,b)$ and $(c,d)$, since the alternative has alternating endpoints on one face and is impossible for disjoint plane paths. Rotating the labels gives the other pairing $(b,c),(d,a)$.

### Returning to the cubic graph

A path in $L$ between terminal vertices corresponds to a sequence of distinct edges in the graph $G[A]$ with its four added leaf edges. Consecutive edges share one vertex. Traverse an intermediate edge when the shared vertices before and after it differ. If they coincide, skip the edge at that vertex. Including the first and last leaf edges gives a trail between the prescribed leaves. It is a path, since revisiting an internal vertex of a trail would need four distinct edges at a vertex of degree at most three. Every edge represented on the line-graph path is either on this root path or incident with it at a skipped edge’s shared vertex. This includes a leaf edge that was represented as an internal vertex of the line-graph path. Two vertex-disjoint line-graph paths give edge-disjoint root paths, and these are vertex-disjoint because their shared internal vertex would again need four incident edges. Dropping the leaf edges therefore gives the requested one or two paths in $G[A]$ that together dominate all its internal edges and visit every requested attachment.

A dominating cycle of $G_0$ crosses the four-edge cut in two or four edges, because both shores have internal edges and a cycle meets a cut an even number of times. With two edges, the restriction on the four-cycle is a path. If its endpoints are adjacent, it must use the longer arc to dominate the edge between the other two vertices. If they are opposite, it uses one of the two other vertices. With four crossings, its restriction is a matching of two adjacent pairs. Apply the corresponding local demand to replace its restriction by paths in $G[A]$. The same endpoint pairs ensure that the result is one cycle. The replacements dominate the internal edges of $G[A]$ and visit all attachments visited before, so the cycle dominates the crossing edges and the unchanged part. This proves the reduction.

## Scope

If a minimum counterexample has a cyclic four-edge cut and a planar cofacial shore with more than four vertices, the replacement gives a smaller counterexample. The argument does not guarantee a cyclic four-edge cut, a planar shore, or a cofacial embedding of its attachments. It applies to planar fragment replacements on any order of graph. The unrestricted conjecture remains open.

From the repository root, run `python3 research/matthews-sumner-conjecture/verify.py`. The replay enumerates simple paths directly in the cubic fragments, checks all 120 local demands across 12 capped fragments, and checks each cap against three-edge deletion. The examples are the graphs obtained by removing a spoke and its ends from eleven prisms and from the dodecahedron. The written proof uses the cited planar Tutte path theorems. A second reader and a formal proof have yet to verify the argument.
