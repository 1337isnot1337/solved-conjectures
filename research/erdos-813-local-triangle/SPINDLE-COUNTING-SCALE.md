# Erdős–Hajnal local triangle conjecture

**A uniform geometric count for possible spindles.** Every split graph on the classical Hermitian unital of order q has at most O(q¹⁷) labeled Moser spindles. This count holds for deterministic choices of the pencil bipartitions. The main point is a uniform O(q³) bound on the number of diamond cores for each pair of opposite tips. This is an upper bound and does not imply that every splitting contains a spindle.

## The two circles argument for a diamond

Fix distinct secants u and v as the nonadjacent tips of a split-graph diamond. Let A,B be its ordered core pair, so that uA,uB,vA,vB,AB must occur. A triple of distinct secants all through one unital point cannot form a triangle in the split graph: their labels lie in the same bipartition.

First suppose the projective intersection X of u and v is outside the unital. A is then determined by the points P₁ on u and Q₁ on v, and B by P₂ on u and Q₂ on v. Each of these points is in the unital, P₂ is different from P₁, and Q₂ is different from Q₁ by the triangle restriction. Fix P₁,Q₁,P₂. Central projection from P₂ maps A projectively onto v. It sends P₁ to X and fixes Q₁. The q+1 unital points on a secant form a Baer subline, meaning a projective image of the copy of the projective line over the order-q subfield. One way to see this is to use an isotropic basis on the nondegenerate Hermitian plane corresponding to the secant. In this basis the affine part of the isotropic locus is a scalar multiple of the kernel of the trace to the order-q subfield, and the other point is at infinity.

The projection of the A-circle is therefore a Baer subline different from the v-circle: it contains X, while the v-circle does not. Two distinct Baer sublines meet in at most two points. Indeed, the automorphism group of the projective line over the subfield is transitive on ordered triples of distinct points. If two sublines contained the same three distinct points, one could choose projectivities taking infinity, zero and one to that same triple in both, and uniqueness over the full field would identify the two sublines. One of the intersection points of our two circles is Q₁. The other, if any, must be Q₂ because the point where A meets B has to be unital. Hence there are at most (q+1)³ ordered possibilities in this case.

Now suppose X is unital. All six pair intersections on the four lines u,v,A,B are unital, and they cannot all be distinct because the classical Hermitian unital has no [O'Nan configuration](https://doi.org/10.4007/annals.2024.199.2.8). Thus at least three of the four are concurrent. The split-graph triangles uAB and vAB exclude concurrence of those triples. Accordingly one of A,B, say A, contains X. The other cannot contain X, since then uAB would be concurrent. Choose B by its distinct intersections with u and v, in at most (q+1)² ways. Choose A by the at most q+1 unital points where it could meet B; a choice specifies a unique line through X. Counting both choices of concurrent core gives at most 2(q+1)³ possibilities.

## Total count and random thinning

Choose the common apex u in O(q⁴) ways and one tip v in O(q⁴) ways. The second tip w must meet v at a unital point, allowing at most (q+1)(q²−1)=O(q³) choices. Choose the two ordered core pairs in O(q³) ways each by the preceding argument. Dropping the disjointness conditions can only increase this upper bound, so there are at most O(q¹⁷) labeled copies.

With independent vertex retention p and independent edge retention s, the expected number of surviving copies is at most O(q¹⁷p⁷s¹¹), for every initial splitting. This singles out a bottleneck for the crude first-moment deletion strategy. Set p=q^(−a) and s=q^(−b), with 0≤a<4 and b≥0. The retained graph has Θ(q^(4−a)) vertices with high probability and expected average degree of order at most q^(3−a−b), by the degree of the unsplit secant graph. The elementary independent-set bound therefore requires b<1−a/2 to obtain a fixed power improvement over the square-root independence scale by this model, even before cleanup. More precisely, when b≥1−a/2, Markov bounds the average degree by q^(2−a/2+o(1)) with high probability, so independence is at least q^(2−a/2−o(1)).

For the displayed upper bound on the expected spindle count to be of smaller order than the vertex count by a power using these exponents, one would instead need 6a+11b>13. But b<1−a/2 implies 6a+11b<11+a/2<13. Thus this uniform count, by itself, cannot justify the first-moment vertex deletion strategy at a square-root beating density. It does not exclude stronger probability estimates, directed edge cleanup, or nonuniform or correlated thinning.
