# Circuit history, arithmetic cancellation, and Galois selection

Status: research. The full Shub--Smale tau conjecture is unresolved. This
file continues [GATE_COUNTS.md](GATE_COUNTS.md); nothing here is
claimed to settle the conjecture or to be novel.

## 1. Removing input rank still leaves an unbounded one-gate increment

For a finite sample set `R` of positive integers, let `Gamma_0` be the
multiplicative subgroup of `Q^*` generated by the input values `R` and
the nonzero constant registers. A proposed refinement of scalar trace
rank might quotient out `Gamma_0`, charging only the extra rank created
by addition gates.

This has no local gate bound independent of the sample set. For any
positive integer `N`, take


```math
R_N=\{2^{2^j}:0\le j\lt N\}.
```


The input group is exactly `<2>`, of rank one. After the single addition
`x+1`, its values are the Fermat numbers


```math
F_j=2^{2^j}+1\qquad(0\le j\lt N).
```


These are odd, greater than one, and pairwise coprime. The identity


```math
\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}F_j=2^{2^k}-1=F_k-2
```


proves pairwise coprimality: any common divisor of `F_i,F_k`, `i\lt k`,
divides two and is odd. Every `F_j` has a prime factor absent from two
and all other `F_i`. Taking the valuation at such a prime shows that
`2,F_0,...,F_{N-1}` are multiplicatively independent. Thus the rank
increment modulo the input group is exactly `N` after one addition.

The same is true of the normalized values `(x+1)/x`: multiplying by
the input `x` does not change their classes modulo `Gamma_0`.

This is an evaluation-table counterexample. It does not refute a bound
that uses the global fact that the samples are roots of the eventual
output: the root polynomial `prod_j (x-2^(2^j))` has an elementary
program of length `O(N)`, consistent with a linear total charge.
Any useful relative-rank amortization must therefore use later
relations in the circuit, rather than bounding the rank increase at
each addition solely from its two parents.

## 2. Unit resultants can coexist with a positive complete history

The positive-pair simulation from the preceding note can be combined
with the unit-resultant obstruction. Let `H=A-B`, where every register
used to build `A,B` is positive on the positive integers, and the
difference is merely a formal representation, not a computed gate.
Form


```math
U=A^2+B^2+1-2AB=H^2+1.
```


The only new subtraction before the final gate outputs `U`, which is
strictly positive on the real line. All intermediate additions and
products are positive on the positive integers. Finally compute


```math
U-1=H^2.
```


Every positive integer root first vanishes at this final gate, whose
parents have resultant `Res(U,1)=1` and have gcd one at every integer
input. The construction requires eight extra gates after the pair
`A,B`: the two squares, their sum, addition of one, the product `AB`,
its doubling, subtraction producing `U`, and the final subtraction.

Using the translation and pair simulation of Proposition 5.2 in
[GATE_COUNTS.md](GATE_COUNTS.md) gives size at most `12s+22` from a length-`s` program,
for `s>=1`. It preserves the number of distinct integer roots and
makes them all positive. Consequently the conjunction of complete
prior nonvanishing, positive prior values, and a unit resultant at
the root-birth gate is still tau-hard.

## 3. Where the factorial enters the additive-complexity bound

In Rojas, arXiv:math/0201154v2, Section 2, the imported arithmetic
multivariate Descartes bound depends on the product of the numbers
`N_i` of variables actually present in the equations. Section 3
uses a first-vanishing additive register and bounds these numbers
by `2,3,...,a,a`; their product produces a factorial.

Thus the parameter to improve is concrete: one needs a sparse
representation of the triangular additive presentation, or a sharper
root theorem for its dependencies, in which the relevant product of
variable counts can be controlled in terms of the original
multiplication history. A bound based only on the count of visible
dependencies is false: those dependencies can be dense after very
few multiplication gates. The next section records the exact example.

Primary source: <https://arxiv.org/html/math/0201154v2>, Arithmetic
Multivariate Descartes' Rule in Section 2 and proof of Theorem 3 in
Section 3. The 2002 final numerical inequality is not disproved here. Its
unrestricted proof dependency is unresolved, as detailed in Section 6
and [GATE_COUNTS.md](GATE_COUNTS.md).

## 4. Dense compressed dependencies from a sparse multiplication history

Set `P_0=x`. For `i=1,...,a`, let


```math
X_i=P_{i-1}+1,
\qquad P_i=P_{i-1}X_i.
```


There are `a` additions and `a` multiplications. After compressing
all multiplications, the additive presentation is


```math
X_i=x\prod_{j\lt i}X_j+1.
```


Its `i`th defining equation involves the `i+1` variables
`x,X_1,...,X_i`, so the variable-count product is factorial in `a`,
although the original DAG has only `2a` gates. In particular the
number of multiplication gates cannot lower-bound the number of
compressed dependency edges, which is quadratic in `a` here.

For this example, retaining the intermediate `P_i` variables does
restore constant arity. Applying a general arithmetic fewnomial
bound then charges the extra variables, however. A valid general
improvement would have to balance these costs; merely using the
original two-parent fan-in does not remove them.

## 5. A square-compression candidate with an explicit source dependency

The following argument gives an exponential base strictly below two
**conditional on an unrestricted arithmetic fewnomial bound**. Its
algebra and circuit reductions have been checked independently, but
Section 6 identifies a gap in the available proof of the imported
bound. Accordingly no unconditional assertion is made here.

### 5.1 The exact imported formula required

The needed assertion is the local case of the displayed
"Arithmetic Multivariate Descartes' Rule (Special Case)" in Section 2
of Rojas, arXiv:math/0201154v2. Specializing to `L=Q_2`, it asserts that
an `n` by `n` system whose `i`th equation has `m_i` monomials and
involves `N_i` variables has at most


```math
(2c)^n\prod_{i=1}^n
 \left\{m_i(m_i-1)N_i
 \left[1+\log_2\frac{m_i-1}{\log 2}\right]\right\},
\qquad c=\frac e{e-1},
\tag{5.1}
```


geometrically isolated roots in `(Q_2^*)^n`, with multiplicities.
The original reference is Rojas, *Arithmetic Multivariate Descartes'
Rule*, arXiv:math/0110327v3, 15 November 2001, Corollary 1 of
Section 2 together with the refined bound near `(1,...,1)`; the
later published version is American Journal of Mathematics 126 (2004),
1--30, DOI <https://doi.org/10.1353/ajm.2004.0005>.

Formula (5.1) is needed for all the systems below, including those
whose tropical hypersurfaces have nontransverse intersections. A
generic-only version does not suffice.

Set


```math
K_0=1+\log_2(1/\log 2),
\qquad L_0=1+\log_2(2/\log 2)=K_0+1.
```


The elementary estimates `c<8/5`, `K_0<8/5`, and `L_0<13/5` give
the following conservative per-equation factors from (5.1):

| equation | monomials | variables | factor less than |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| earlier addition | 3 | 3 | `36 c L_0 < 150` |
| earlier product | 2 | 3 | `12 c K_0 < 31` |
| last addition, set to zero | 2 | 2 | `8 c K_0 < 21` |

The sharper constants `30` and `20` are also valid: `log 2>69/100`
implies `K_0<31/20`, because `100/69<2^(11/20)`; together with
`c<8/5` this gives `12cK_0<29.76<30` and `8cK_0<19.84<20`.
The inequality `100/69<2^(11/20)` is an exact integer check
`100^20<2^11 69^20`. The bound `log 2>69/100` follows, for example,
from the first two terms of
`log 2 = 2 sum_{j>=0} ((2j+1) 3^(2j+1))^(-1)`:
`2/3+2/81=56/81>69/100`.

### 5.2 Semantic preprocessing, with no cost increase

Given the original circuit, process its registers in order and
represent each by either zero or a sign times a retained nonzero
polynomial register. If an operation produces the zero polynomial,
retain no new register. An addition or subtraction with a zero
operand is a signed copy and likewise needs no new register. Products
with a zero operand are zero. Signs are absorbed into the two
coefficients of a later addition, or into a sign tag on a product.

Every other original gate creates at most one retained gate. The
result is a proof representation, not a proposed efficient identity
testing algorithm. It starts with `1,x`, has length at most `s`, all
its polynomial registers are nonzero, and its output equals the
original output up to a sign. Each retained gate is a signed sum of
two earlier nonzero registers or their product. Signed edges do not
affect degrees or roots, and (5.1) allows these rational coefficients.

Let `a` count retained addition/subtraction gates, `q` count products
whose two parent register identifiers agree, and `b` count products
of two distinct parent identifiers. Thus `a+b+q<=s`. Equality of
polynomial values at distinct registers does not change this
definition: distinct identifiers still count separately.

### 5.3 Compress only squaring chains

Remove the `q` squaring gates as named variables, replacing each
maximal chain by an integral power. Each remaining parent expression
is a power of either `x` or one earlier retained non-square register,
or is a constant. Thus the `a+b` retained gate equations have the
forms


```math
Y_i-c_1U^{d_1}-c_2V^{d_2}=0
\quad\hbox{or}\quad
Y_i-U^{d_1}V^{d_2}=0,
\tag{5.2}
```


with nonnegative integral exponents, nonzero scalar coefficients,
and `U,V` each one earlier variable or a constant.
Each equation involves at most three variables; there are at most
three terms for an addition and two for a product. Coincident
variables or combined monomials only reduce these numbers.

Consider a nonzero `Q_2` root of the output. Because every retained
polynomial is nonzero, there is a first retained register that
vanishes there. It must be an addition: a product or a pure power
cannot first vanish while its parents are nonzero.

Suppose its position among retained registers is `ell`, and it is
the `j`th addition. Introduce the `ell` variables
`x,Y_1,...,Y_(ell-1)`. Use the earlier `ell-1` equations (5.2),
and use the two-term expression defining `Y_ell` as the last
equation, set equal to zero. The relevant root maps to a torus root
because `x` and every earlier register are nonzero.

This map is injective, and its image consists of geometrically
isolated roots. Indeed the earlier equations are triangular, each
monic of degree one in its newly introduced variable. Eliminating
them identifies the full quotient with `Q_2[x]/(H_ell(x))`, where
`H_ell` is the nonzero polynomial computed by the birth register.
The quotient is finite dimensional; localization to the torus
preserves zero dimensionality. The same argument after algebraic
closure verifies the word "geometrically". If the last expression
has only one surviving monomial, it has no torus roots and can be
discarded.

Assuming (5.1), the number of these roots is less than
`20 * 150^(j-1) * 30^b`. Summing over the `a` possible additions
and allowing the possible input root zero gives


```math
N(f)\le 1+20\,30^b\frac{150^a-1}{149}
\le 150^a30^b\qquad(a\ge1).
\tag{5.3}
```


When `a=0`, every register is a monomial and `N(f)<=1`, so the
last bound still holds.

### 5.4 A degree bound distinguishing squares from other products

Let `phi=(1+sqrt(5))/2`, `theta=1/phi`, and maintain the two largest
degrees `M>=S` among all registers, with one additional virtual copy
of the input `x`. Degrees count register multiplicity, so initially
`M=S=1`. The invariant `S>=M/2` holds throughout: if a new maximum
is introduced, its degree is at most twice the old maximum, which
then remains available as the second-largest degree.

Put `P=M+theta S`. An addition changes `P` by a factor at most
`phi`, since its output degree is at most `M`. A product of distinct
register identifiers has degree at most `M+S`. If it introduces a
new maximum, then


```math
P'\le M+S+\theta M=\phi P;
```


otherwise `P'<=phi M<=phi P`. A square has output degree at most
`2M`; if it introduces a new maximum, then


```math
P'\le(2+\theta)M\le2P,
```


where the last inequality uses `S>=M/2`. If it does not introduce
a new maximum the factor two is again sufficient. Therefore


```math
P\le\phi\,2^q\phi^{a+b}.
```


Since `P>=M(1+theta/2)=(phi^2/2)M`,


```math
\deg f\le \frac2\phi\,2^q\phi^{a+b}.
\tag{5.4}
```


The usual degree bound also gives `deg f<=2^(q+b)`.
These are unconditional circuit facts.

### 5.5 Conditional optimization

For `N(f)>=1`, put `z=log_2 N(f)`,


```math
\delta=\log_2(2/\phi),\quad
A=\log_2 150,\quad B=\log_2 30,\quad
K=A-B+B/\delta.
```


The two unconditional degree bounds and the conditional (5.3)
give respectively


```math
z\le s-a,\qquad
z\le s-\delta(a+b)+\delta,\qquad
z\le Aa+Bb.
```


Because `A>B>0`, substitute the first two inequalities into the
last one:


```math
z\le (A-B)(s-z)+(B/\delta)(s-z+\delta)
=K(s-z)+B.
```


Consequently, **if (5.1) holds for every required system**, then


```math
N(f)\le 2^{B/(K+1)}\left(2^{K/(K+1)}\right)^s.
\tag{5.5}
```


Numerically the prefactor is about `1.191949` and the exponential
base about `1.929697`. These decimal values only describe the
exact formula; they are not used as proved rounded inequalities.
This would still be exponential, and would not prove tau or the
desired superpolynomial lower bound for consecutive-root multiples.

## 6. Source audit: the missing tropical hypothesis

The proof in arXiv:math/0110327v3, Section 2, asserts that the number
of valuation vectors of isolated roots is at most the product of
the numbers of edges of the individual Newton polytopes. That
assertion fails in a tropically degenerate intersection. For
example, over `C_2`, consider


```math
Y-1-X=0,\qquad Y^{2^t}-1=0.
\tag{6.1}
```


The first Newton polytope has three edges and the second has one.
Nevertheless, the nonzero torus roots include
`(X,Y)=(zeta-1,zeta)` for primitive roots of unity of orders
`2,4,...,2^t`. Their valuation vectors are


```math
(1,0),(1/2,0),\ldots,(1/2^{t-1},0).
```


These are isolated and distinct. The shifted-cyclotomic calculation
in the preceding note proves the valuation values. Thus the
edge-product assertion fails already when `t>3`.

This alone is not a counterexample to the numerical `Q_2` root
bound (5.1): only the order-two nontrivial root of unity belongs
to `Q_2`. It is a gap in the displayed proof, and the current
argument cannot fill it by invoking that proof.

Koiran--Portier--Rojas, arXiv:1309.0486v1, Definition 1.9 and the
paragraph following it, explicitly distinguish tropically generic
valuation counts from general ones, stating that Smirnov's theorem
gives the former. The 2026 paper *New Explicit Root Counts for
p-adic Circuit Systems*, DOI <https://doi.org/10.1145/3832046.3832061>,
also describes previous explicit multivariate bounds as requiring
tropical genericity. The exact scope of a repaired published
version and any independent proof of the 2002 additive-complexity
theorem must therefore be checked before (5.5), or the earlier
2002-based gate-count optimization, is called unconditional.

## 7. Why the immediate genericity repairs fail

### 7.1 Rescaling and field extension

Replacing torus variables by `x_i=c_i y_i` translates all their
valuation vectors by the same fixed vector `(v_2(c_i))`. More
generally an invertible monomial change of torus coordinates induces
an invertible linear change of valuation space. Such changes preserve
the dimensions and transversality of the intersections of tropical
hypersurfaces. They cannot repair a nontransverse intersection.

Passing from `Q_2` to a finite extension also does not change the
tropical hypersurfaces in the algebraically closed field `C_2`.
It changes which valuation vectors are realized by roots in the
chosen local field, not the geometric transversality assumption.

### 7.2 Small coefficient changes

For a fixed finite support, changing every nonzero coefficient by
an amount of valuation strictly greater than that coefficient's
valuation preserves all coefficient valuations. Hence it preserves
the lifted Newton polytope and its tropical hypersurface exactly.
Thus small perturbations that preserve simple local roots need
not make the system tropically generic.

There is a direct test for the proposed perturbation `H+epsilon H'`.
Take `H(x)=(1+x)^D-1` with `D=2^t`. With `Y=1+X`, the equations are


```math
Y-1-X=0,\qquad
Y^D-1+\epsilon D Y^{D-1}=0.
```


If `v_2(epsilon D)>0`, every weight `(u,0)` with `u>0` lies in both
tropical hypersurfaces. In the first equation the constant and `Y`
terms tie; in the second the constant and `Y^D` terms tie, while
the derivative term has strictly larger valuation. These give a
nontransverse ray. The example already had only simple algebraic
roots before perturbation, so a simple-root promise does not fix it.

### 7.3 Translation and the sparsity cost

An affine translation can change tropical genericity, but its cost
must be charged. Expanding `(X+c)^D` for `c!=0` in characteristic
zero gives `D+1` nonzero terms. In a compressed-square circuit,
`D` can be exponential in the original program length.

Introducing a helper variable `Z=X+c` keeps the power expression
sparse but does not, by itself, remove a nontransverse intersection
already present in the old variables. Every old tropical weight
has at least one lift compatible with the tropical linear relation:
the valuation for the new variable can be chosen as the minimum of
the old-variable and constant valuations (with the usual tie ray
when they are equal). A different set of defining equations might
do better, but a bound on its support and construction cost is
missing. Merely adjoining translated coordinate variables is not
such a bound.

## 8. An unconditional Q_2 simplification at a birth gate

Although it does not resolve the genericity problem, square
compression has a useful exact arithmetic consequence. Suppose
`u,v` are nonzero elements of `Q_2` and `0<=k<=ell`. If `k>=1`,
then


```math
u^{2^k}+v^{2^\ell}\ne0,
```


and


```math
u^{2^k}-v^{2^\ell}=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
u=\pm v^{2^{\ell-k}}.
\tag{8.1}
```


Indeed, `-1` is not a square in `Q_2`: a putative square root is
a unit, but every odd square is one modulo eight. Thus a positive
even-power sum cannot vanish. If `w^(2^k)=1`, repeatedly factor
`w^(2^j)-1`; the only possible power-of-two roots of unity in
`Q_2` are `1,-1`, again because `-1` is not a square. Apply this
to `w=u/v^(2^(ell-k))` to obtain (8.1).

There is also no uncontrolled valuation gain for the plus case.
When `k,ell>=1` and the two displayed powers have equal valuation,
factoring out that common power of two leaves two odd even powers,
both one modulo eight. Their sum has valuation exactly one.
Thus


```math
v_2(u^{2^k}+v^{2^\ell})
=\begin{cases}
\min\{2^k v_2(u),2^\ell v_2(v)\},&
2^k v_2(u)\ne2^\ell v_2(v),\\
2^k v_2(u)+1,&
2^k v_2(u)=2^\ell v_2(v).
\end{cases}
```


For the minus case with equal valuations, put
`a=u/2^(v_2(u))` and
`b=v^(2^(ell-k))/2^(v_2(u))`, both units. The standard factorization
into `a-b`, `a+b`, and higher even-power sums proves directly


```math
v_2(a^{2^k}-b^{2^k})
=v_2(a-b)+v_2(a+b)+k-1
\quad(k\ge1),
```


when the difference is nonzero. Each higher sum contributes exactly
one by the preceding argument.

At a signed birth gate these observations remove a common long
squaring chain at the price of at most two rational branches. They
leave equations with one unsquared parent, such as
`U=V^(2^j)`. If `U,V` depend on shared prior additions, their
integer solutions are not bounded here. This is the precise
remaining case for this local simplification.

## 9. A real-root bound for the remaining power comparison

Let `U,V in R[x]` be nonzero, let `d=deg U`, `e=deg V`, let
`D>=1` be an integer, and let `sigma!=0` be real. If
`F=U-sigma V^D` is nonzero, then


```math
\#Z_{\mathbb R}(F)\le d+2e.
\tag{9.1}
```


Proof. Put `W=U'V-DUV'`. If `W=0`, the rational function
`U/V^D` is constant. Since `F!=0`, every root of `F` is a root
of `V`, so there are at most `e` roots. Otherwise `deg W<=d+e-1`.
Let `c` be the number of common real roots of `U,V`, and `v` the
number of distinct real roots of `V`. Every common root is a root
of `W`, so `W` has at most `d+e-1-c` roots outside `Z(V)`.
The complement of `Z(V)` consists of `v+1` intervals. On each
interval apply Rolle's theorem to `U/V^D-sigma`, whose derivative
is `W/V^(D+1)`. The number of its zeros is at most the number of
zeros of `W` there plus one. Thus the noncommon roots of `F`
number at most `d+e-1-c+v+1`, and adding the `c` common roots
gives at most `d+e+v<=d+2e`.

This eliminates the last exponent from a root bound without any
local-field assumption. Its limitation is concrete: `d,e` may
already be exponential in the length of the shared prefix. The
Rolle argument also does not preserve integrality of intermediate
critical points. For example `x^3-x` has three rational roots,
whereas `3x^2-1` has no root in `Q_2`, since `1/3` is not a
square in `Q_2` (it is three modulo eight). A direct rational or
`Q_2` replacement for this real Rolle step is therefore false.

## 10. What polynomial abc does and does not provide

For completeness, the polynomial abc inequality used in this
attempt has a short proof. Let `A+B=C` be pairwise coprime nonzero
polynomials over a characteristic-zero field, not all constant,
and let `R=rad(ABC)`. The polynomial
`W=A'B-AB'` is nonzero. At a root of `A`, `B`, or `C` of
multiplicity `m`, `W` has multiplicity at least `m-1`; for `C`
use `W=C'B-CB'`. Pairwise coprimality therefore gives
`ABC/R | W`. Comparing degrees yields
`deg C<=deg R-1`, and permuting the roles of `A,B,C` gives


```math
\max\{\deg A,\deg B,\deg C\}\le\deg\mathop{\mathrm{rad}}(ABC)-1.
```


Apply this with `A=U`, `B=-V^D`, `C=H=U-V^D`, assuming
`gcd(U,V)=1`. The result includes


```math
D\deg V\le\deg\mathop{\mathrm{rad}}U+
\deg\mathop{\mathrm{rad}}V+
\deg\mathop{\mathrm{rad}}H-1.
\tag{10.1}
```


Thus the inequality forces many distinct algebraic roots when a
large power cannot be absorbed by the other factors. It supplies
no upper bound on the number of rational linear factors: those
factors contribute positively to its right side. A rational-root
estimate would require additional arithmetic information that
distinguishes rational roots from the other simple roots, rather
than only coprimality, degrees, or multiplicities.

## 11. Universal simultaneous Galois fixed-point selection

### Lemma 11.1 (elementary finite-group statement)

Let a finite group `G` act on a finite set `Omega`, with
`|Omega|=D`. Let `r` be the number of points fixed by all of `G`,
and let the other orbits have sizes `d_1,...,d_m>=2`.
Choose `g_1,...,g_k` independently and uniformly from `G`, where
`k>=1`. Then


```math
\mathbb E\left|\bigcap_{j=1}^k\mathop{\mathrm{Fix}}(g_j)\right|
=r+\sum_{i=1}^m d_i^{1-k}
\le r+\frac{D-r}{2^k}.
\tag{11.1}
```


Proof. A point in an orbit of size `d` has stabilizer of index
`d`, hence has probability `1/d` of being fixed by a uniform
group element. Independence gives probability `d^(-k)` of
surviving all `k` choices. Sum these indicator expectations over
the `d` points of each orbit. For each nonsingleton orbit,
`d*d^(-k)<=d*2^(-k)`, proving the inequality.

In particular, if `2^k>D-r`, some tuple has no surviving
nonfixed points. The conclusion follows because the expected
number of such points is strictly less than one and is a
nonnegative integer for every tuple. More quantitatively, the
probability of retaining any nonfixed point is at most
`(D-r)/2^k`, by Markov's inequality.

There is also a greedy proof. For any subset `S` of nonfixed
points, the average over `g in G` of `|S intersect Fix(g)|` is
`sum_{omega in S} 1/|G omega|<=|S|/2`. Some choice of `g`
therefore halves the current survivors. Iterate until none remain.

### Corollary 11.2 (arbitrary polynomial and arbitrary cofactors)

Let `f in Q[x]` be nonzero, let `Omega` be its distinct roots in
an algebraic closure, and let `G` be the Galois group of its
splitting field. The points fixed by all of `G` are exactly the
rational roots. If `f` has a length-`s` program in the target
model, then `D<=2^s`, and `s+1` group elements suffice to leave
exactly its rational roots as their common fixed points. A
uniform independent tuple of that length succeeds with probability
at least one half. Constant polynomials have an empty root set
and are immediate.

This result needs no restriction on the root locations and no
factorization promise. In particular it applies to every nonzero
multiple of a prescribed integer-root polynomial. Unknown
cofactors simply add further Galois orbits. Rational roots of
the cofactor also survive; nonrational ones can be removed.
The lemma selects rational roots, so an upper bound on the
selected set would also bound integer roots. It does not assert
that every selected rational root is integral.

### 11.3 Realization by primes is a separate imported step

Chebotarev's density theorem realizes every conjugacy class in
`G` as the Frobenius class at infinitely many unramified rational
primes. A precise source is J. S. Milne, *Algebraic Number Theory*,
version 3.08, 19 July 2020, Theorem 8.31:
<https://jmilne.org/math/CourseNotes/ANT.pdf>.

For each selected `g_j`, choose a distinct unramified prime with
its conjugacy class. Choosing a prime ideal of the splitting
field above it selects a representative of that class; conjugating
the prime ideal realizes the desired representative `g_j`.
Primes in any finite bad set can be excluded. This establishes an
existential prime realization of the group lemma.

No effective bound on the primes, their bit lengths, or their
constant-free construction costs is claimed. Nor does a list of
separate finite-field root sets supply their common fixed-point
set: the roots must be identified consistently inside one
splitting field. Conjugacy classes alone do not specify those
identifications. These costs and this identification issue are
separate from the elementary averaging argument.

### 11.4 A fixed palette of primes cannot replace adaptive selection

Let `P` be any finite set of primes. Put
`Q=4 prod_{p in P, p odd} p` and `A=Q^2+1`. The polynomial


```math
g(x)=x^2-A
```


has no rational root: `Q^2\lt A<(Q+1)^2`. But it has both roots
in `Q_p` for every `p in P`. For odd `p`, `A=1 mod p` and
the roots `+1,-1` lift by the elementary simple-root Hensel
argument. At two, `A=1 mod 8`, so it is a square in `Q_2`.
One can prove this last statement constructively: starting with
an odd square root modulo eight, at precision `2^n`, `n>=3`,
one of `u` and `u+2^(n-1)` corrects the next bit of the square,
because their squares differ by `2^n u mod 2^(n+1)`.

Thus all these local tests accept two nonrational algebraic roots.
In the quadratic splitting field every prime in the palette
splits, so all corresponding Frobenius elements are the identity.
The selection lemma correctly requires choices depending on the
polynomial. This example does not contradict the lemma or tau.

### 11.5 What remains for a circuit invariant

For every register polynomial `h_i`, the evaluation map
`h_i:Omega -> Qbar` is `G`-equivariant. Rational input roots
give rational values at every register. The map `h_x` is the
identity on `Omega`, so the common-fixed subset of the full
trace is already the rational-root subset at the input; it does
not arise as a newly growing subset at later gates.

Individual register maps can collapse arbitrarily many nontrivial
orbits to distinct rational values in one gate. For distinct
positive nonsquares `a_1,...,a_N`, take
`Omega={+sqrt(a_i),-sqrt(a_i):1<=i<=N}`. The map `x^2` sends
its nonsingleton Galois orbits to the `N` rational values `a_i`.
The maps `u=x` and `v=x^2-x` have no rational values there, but
`u+v=x^2` does. This is an evaluation-map obstruction, not a
short-program construction for the polynomial with all these
roots.

A successful joint invariant must use the vanishing relation at
the output together with the entire equivariant history. Bounding
orbit collapse at an individual gate, or counting fixed points
separately for each Frobenius, loses that requirement. The
selection lemma removes the extra-orbit contribution from the
single-prime average, but it does not supply a gate bound for
the common fixed set.
