# All ambiguities in a generic tree's unlabelled leaf distances

A reduced metric tree has no vertices of degree two and has strictly positive edge lengths. Its leaf data are the multiset of distances between unordered pairs of distinct leaves. For a fixed target topology, take target lengths outside the finitely many rational hyperplanes specified in the [crossing-array theorem](LEAF-DECK-LENGTH.md). Rational independence of the lengths suffices. Every candidate competing tree below may have arbitrary positive lengths.

For at least four leaves the following operations apply. With two or three leaves the metric tree is unique up to isometry, as shown below.

**Classification.** Every reduced metric tree with the same leaf data can be obtained by the following operations on the target, up to a weighted tree isomorphism.

1. Choose any binary refinement of the target and assign length zero to the new internal edges. A cherry is a pair of leaves with the same parent in this refinement. For each cherry record the sum of its two pendant lengths.
2. Permute those sums among the cherries. If a cherry receives a new sum differing by twice a number $`t`$, add $`t`$ to both its pendant lengths and subtract $`t`$ from its stem, the edge from the parent towards the rest of the tree. When two cherries share one stem, apply both adjustments there.
3. Retain choices giving positive pendant lengths and nonnegative internal lengths. Contract all internal edges of length zero.

Conversely every such choice has the same leaf data. In particular a binary target, after also excluding the finite stem equalities below, determines its unlabelled combinatorial topology as well as its total length, and its entire ambiguity in the metric is finite. For a binary target with $`c`$ cherries there are at most $`c!`$ weighted isometry types. For general targets the sum of these bounds over binary refinements is finite. These bounds concern the observed pairwise distances alone.

## Corresponding internal skeletons

For now assume there are $`n\ge4`$ leaves. The [crossing-array proof](LEAF-DECK-LENGTH.md) supplies binary refinements of the rival and target and a bijection of their internal edge sets. A leaf pair crosses an internal edge in the rival exactly when the matched target pair of equal distance crosses the associated target internal edge. Across each individual internal edge, the matching of leaf pairs is the product of a bijection on each side of that cut. Let the internal skeleton be the tree induced by the internal vertices of a binary refinement. A pendant group at a skeleton vertex consists of its incident leaves. Its size is three minus the degree in the skeleton.

An unlabelled tree isomorphism of the two skeletons realizes the internal edge bijection. To see this, for each pair of leaves keep the set of skeleton edges along the path joining them. These sets correspond exactly on the two sides. Distinct edges $`e,f`$ fail to be adjacent precisely when there is a third edge $`g`$ in every such set containing both $`e,f`$. For disjoint edges choose an intervening edge. For adjacent edges, a third edge branches off at their common vertex or lies beyond one outer end. In the latter case choose an outward leaf through a different branch at that end, which exists by trivalence in the full refinement. One can therefore join two leaves through $`e,f`$ while avoiding that specified $`g`$. This recovers the adjacency relation of the skeleton edges, including the cases of one or two edges.

For a skeleton with at least two edges, the maximal sets of pairwise adjacent edges of size at least two are exactly the edge sets incident to skeleton vertices of degree at least two. Indeed three pairwise adjacent edges in a tree all meet at the same vertex. The remaining endpoints are leaves of the skeleton, reconstructed by appending a new endpoint at each edge for each of its missing incidences. For a single edge choose either identification of its two endpoints. This constructs the required skeleton isomorphism. The group sizes hence agree at corresponding vertices.

If the matched path between a pair of different pendant groups has at least two skeleton edges, the ordered pair of its endpoint groups up to reversal is recovered from that set of edges as its two endpoints. The same holds when the path has one edge, whose two ends are the two group vertices. Thus the distance multisets between any two specified distinct groups agree. Pairs with no skeleton edges are exactly the within-cherry pairs, one per group of size two. Their distances are the unordered multiset of cherry sums in step 2.

## One leaf bijection outside the within-cherry pairs

Assume first $`n\ge5`$. A cherry sits at a leaf of the skeleton. Its stem split has sides of sizes two and $`n-2\gt2`$. On its size-two side, the crossing-array product matches the two leaves to the corresponding target cherry by a single bijection, valid for every outside mate. At every singleton group there is only one possible leaf match. Combine these choices into one global leaf bijection $`\phi`$. A pair in different groups has its target endpoint groups prescribed by its nonempty internal-edge chain. At a cherry endpoint its particular target leaf is prescribed by that cherry's crossing array; at a singleton it is forced by its parent. Therefore the match of every pair from different groups is exactly the pair given by $`\phi`$. For $`n=4`$ there is one internal edge. Choose its orientation and the two leaf bijections to agree with the product description of its $`2\times2`$ crossing array. This gives the same conclusion for the quartet.

Use $`\phi`$ to identify the two full binary refinements as combinatorial trees. Between different groups, every labelled distance already agrees. The remaining distances, one within each cherry, are an unordered multiset of sums, so there is a permutation assigning each target cherry its observed rival value. Perform step 2 on the target using this permutation, provisionally allowing signed adjusted lengths. In a path leaving a cherry, the change to its pendant is canceled by the opposite change to its stem. This remains true for a shared stem, whose adjustment is the sum of both changes. The new within-cherry distance is its assigned rival value. Thus *every* labelled distance now agrees.

The length of every edge of a fixed binary topology is recovered linearly from its labelled leaf distances, even for signed or zero lengths. For a pendant $`i`$, choose leaves $`j,k`$ through the two other directions at its parent and take half of $`d_{ij}+d_{ik}-d_{jk}`$. For an internal edge choose two leaves on either side through the separate directions at its respective endpoints. Half the difference between a crossing sum and the noncrossing sum is its length. Consequently the provisional adjusted lengths agree one by one with the rival refinement's actual lengths. In particular, they satisfy step 3. Conversely, any feasible transformation in steps 1–3 fixes every intergroup distance and permutes the within-cherry values. Total length is preserved since the changes to all cherry means sum to zero.

For a rationally independent binary target no adjusted stem can vanish. When the stem is distinct its original length plus half a difference of two cherry sums is a nonzero rational linear form in the target edge lengths. In the quartet the shared stem length never changes. For the topology conclusion under the finite-hyperplane formulation, also exclude the finitely many nonidentical equalities between a stem length and a half-difference of two cherry sums. All rivals then retain the target's unlabelled binary topology. At $`n=3`$ the pendant lengths are the three half-perimeter differences and every permutation of the observed distances comes from relabelling leaves. At $`n=2`$ there is a single edge. The cherry operation is unnecessary in these two cases.

For a rationally independent binary target with at least four leaves, the bound of $`c!`$ is sharp and the number of weighted isometry types is exactly the number of feasible permutations. The absolute differences of its two pendant lengths at the different cherries are pairwise distinct by independence. Every cherry adjustment preserves its own difference. Thus any isometry between two adjusted trees must match each cherry to the one with the same difference, and its within-cherry sum determines the assigned value. Distinct permutations give distinct isometry types. On each fixed binary topology all permutations are simultaneously feasible on an open set: take all pendant lengths in a sufficiently short interval around one and all internal edges longer than that interval's width. Positive rationally independent length vectors exist in this set.

## Complete weighted reconstruction without trivalent cherries

Suppose there are at least five leaves and the target has no degree-three vertex incident to two leaves. Then its generic leaf-distance multiset determines its entire weighted metric tree among all positive reduced trees. This applies, for example, whenever every internal degree is at least four. In any binary refinement every cherry stem has length zero. A positive stem would be an original target edge whose split has exactly two leaves on one side, which in a reduced tree forces that side to consist of a trivalent parent and its two leaves. Each cherry has a distinct stem because there are at least five leaves. Feasibility at a zero stem forces the shift of its two pendants to be nonpositive. All shifts sum to zero under a permutation of the sums, so they all vanish. Contracting the inserted edges recovers the original weighted tree.

## Source scope

Pauplin's length formula and the known reconstruction from labelled leaf matrices apply in the [companion argument](LEAF-DECK-LENGTH.md). The bare multiset in this classification omits all leaf incidences. Evans and Lanoue exhibit unweighted trees of different shapes with the same leaf-distance distribution and establish a generic reconstruction theorem from the stronger joint sequence of random subtree lengths. The binary subdivision and cherry-sum arguments above require only the two-leaf multiset and specify the resulting metric ambiguity. [Evans and Lanoue](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6135540/)

When both the target and rival are stars, their pair distances are the ordinary two-element subset sums of their pendant lengths. Selfridge and Straus, Theorem 1, already prove uniqueness in that problem when the cardinality is not a power of two. Their Theorem 3 classifies universal transformations preserving subset sums and gives the exceptional four-number transformation for pair sums. This is prior work on the star-to-star case. Here a rival of arbitrary tree topology is allowed and the described cherry assignments account for all possibilities on every fixed target topology. [Selfridge and Straus, 1958, §§2–3](https://msp.org/pjm/1958/8-4/pjm-v8-n4-p17-s.pdf)
