# Exact and approximate length on flat tori

These give quantitative bounds in the torus direction of [Steinerberger's problem](https://faculty.washington.edu/steinerb/openproblems.pdf#page=39). The 2019 article proves the dimension-two smooth and band-limited bounds and remarks that higher-dimensional variants exist; the results below distinguish exact smooth attainment from stable approximate maximization. The sharp smooth inequality in dimension two is not claimed.

## Exact length is unstable in dimensions at least three

On the standard d-torus, d≥3, consider

    f_N(x) = Σ_{j=1}^{d−2} cos(2πx_j)
             + exp(−N²) cos(2π(N x_{d−1}+x_d)).

These smooth mean-zero functions converge in C∞ to a nonzero function. They even have uniformly bounded holomorphic extensions on each fixed-width complex strip. The closed geodesic from the origin whose primitive direction is (0,…,0,1,−N) sees every cosine equal to 1, so the exact absolute supremum is d−2+exp(−N²). Any direction attaining it must be orthogonal to every listed frequency, because averaging kills a nonorthogonal mode and the remaining sum is then strictly smaller in magnitude. The only primitive directions annihilating all the frequencies are this direction and its negative. The shortest exact maximizer therefore has length sqrt(1+N²).

Thus there is no locally bounded exact length estimate in the C∞ topology away from the zero function, even on real-analytic data, nor a uniform bound based on finitely many upper derivative norms and a lower nonzero L² norm. This is instability of exact maximization, not failure of qualitative attainment.

## Sharp band limit

If a trigonometric polynomial on the standard d-torus has all frequencies of Euclidean norm ≤R, R≥1, an exact maximizing geodesic has length O_d(R^(d−1)), and the exponent is sharp for every d≥2.

Indeed take a maximizing direction v, using [attainment](ATTAINMENT.md), and let A be the span of the retained frequencies. From r≤d−1 independent integer rows of rank r with coordinates bounded by R, choose a nonsingular r-by-r minor. Include any one further column, take the signed r-minors giving a kernel vector of that r-by-(r+1) matrix, and set other coordinates to zero. This produces a nonzero integer vector w annihilating A of norm O_d(R^r). The case r=0 uses a unit coordinate vector. Dividing by the gcd of its coordinates can only shorten it. The identities of Fourier projections give P_v P_w f=P_v f, since every frequency retained by P_v lies in A and is retained by P_w. Hence ||P_w f||∞≥||P_v f||∞, so w is also maximizing.

For sharpness take the cosine sum with frequencies N e_i−e_{i+1}, 1≤i≤d−1, all of norm ≤N+1. Its exact maximal average d−1 at base point 0 requires every mode to survive. The common annihilator is the line generated by the primitive vector (1,N,…,N^(d−1)), so its shortest length is of order N^(d−1).

## Approximate replacement for Hölder data

For 0<α≤1, there is an ε-near-maximizing geodesic of length

    O_{d,α}(max{1, [f]_{C^α}/ε}^((d−1)/α)).

Approximate f in uniform norm by a polynomial of band limit O_d(R) with error O_{d,α}([f]_{C^α}R^(−α)). For example convolve with the product of one-dimensional normalized Jackson kernels (sin(πRt)/sin(πt))⁴. The normalization factor has order R³, by the estimates |sin(πRt)/sin(πt)|≤C min(R,|t|⁻¹) and a matching lower bound for |t|≤c/R. The normalized kernel has α-moment O(R^(−α)); expand the product and use the Hölder seminorm. Its Fourier support has bounded multiple of R in each coordinate, and convolution is defined periodically. Choose R to make the uniform error at most ε/2 and apply the band-limit bound. Both the maximal average and the average along the selected direction change by at most the error.

The exponent cannot be decreased for an absolute-error bound in terms of the Hölder seminorm: scale the sharp band-limit example by N^(−α). Its Hölder seminorm stays bounded by the cosine difference estimate min(2,CN dist(x,y)), and missing even one frequency drops the supremal possible absolute average by at least N^(−α). Take ε<N^(−α)/2.
