# Divisibility after a branching prefix

Consider a deterministic automaton for a relation on pairs of base-$`k`$ integers $`(A,B)`$, read most significant digit first. We ask for $`B>0`$ and $`B\mid A`$. Retain just the transitions reading a zero on the $`B`$ track. Call a state branching if it has two distinctly labelled outgoing edges inside its strongly connected component in this graph. A marked word passes through such a state while all divisor digits read so far are zero. A visit to the initial state counts. [Shallit’s question](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~shallit/Fields/shallit.pdf)

**Decision theorem.** For every base, existence of a marked witness with $`\gcd(B,k)=1`$ is decidable. For a prime-power base, existence of a marked witness is decidable for arbitrary positive $`B`$. In both cases the test is ordinary finite-automaton emptiness, with an effective construction from the input automaton.

Fix a branching state $`s`$. Follow each of its two internal edges back to $`s`$, giving closed digit words $`u,v`$ starting in different digits. The words $`uv`$ and $`vu`$ are distinct and have equal length $`d`$. Denote their numerical values by $`a,b`$, in the order $`a\lt b`$, and put $`C=b-a>0`$. Distinct equal-length words remain distinct as integers even when they start with zero. Both blocks return to $`s`$ with zero divisor digits.

Take any accepted word with a specified visit to $`s`$ of the required kind. Inserting either block at the visit acts on the current numerator prefix $`x`$ as $`x\mapsto k^d x+a`$ or $`x\mapsto k^d x+b`$. Modulo any $`M`$ coprime to $`k`$, these are permutations. The monoid they generate on this finite set contains their inverses. Composing the second with the inverse of the first gives translation by $`C`$. Consequently insertions can change the prefix by any multiple of $`C`$ modulo $`M`$. The suffix of length $`\ell`$ multiplies this change by $`k^\ell`$, a unit modulo $`M`$. The denominator is unchanged.

If $`B`$ is coprime to $`k`$, inspect candidate marked words for the single additional condition $`\gcd(C,B)\mid A`$. It is necessary for a witness. It is sufficient for existence because the permitted insertions can make the numerator zero modulo $`B`$. The test tracks both inputs modulo the fixed $`C`$ and tracks the zero-divisor prefix and the visit. Take the finite union over branching states.

If $`k=p^e`$, write $`B=p^h M`$ with $`\gcd(M,k)=1`$. Add the regular condition $`v_p(A)\ge v_p(B)`$, including $`A=0`$. In base $`p^e`$ it compares trailing zero digits, breaking a tie by the $`p`$-valuation of the last nonzero digit, an integer in $`[0,e-1]`$. Equivalently, recognize this condition on reversed input and reverse the resulting regular language. Because $`0\lt B\lt k^\ell`$ on a marked word, $`p^h\mid k^\ell`$. Thus every insertion preserves $`A`$ modulo $`p^h`$. The condition $`\gcd(C,B)\mid A`$ lets the same translations correct the residue modulo $`M`$. Both prime components now divide the new numerator. This again proves necessity and sufficiency for existence.

In these settings the remaining decision problem can be restricted to paths avoiding all branching states until the first nonzero divisor digit. After those states are removed, every nontrivial strongly connected component of the zero-divisor graph is a directed cycle. Indeed each vertex has at most one internal outgoing edge in any surviving component. Thus every remaining prefix belongs to one of finitely many patterns of fixed connecting words and powers of fixed words, with the number of powers bounded by the number of states. The suffix is still arbitrary. The reduction imposes no restriction on the prime factors of the quotient.
